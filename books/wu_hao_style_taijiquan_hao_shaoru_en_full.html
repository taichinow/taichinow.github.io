A Short Account of Wu-Style Taijiquan

A Short Account of Wu-Style Taijiquan
in a dark front-buttoned jacket.

Wǔ Héqīng (武河清) of Yongnian in Hebei, courtesy name Yǔxiāng (禹襄, 1812–1880), came from a minor official-landlord family, and he and his two brothers practised martial arts at home. When Yáng Lùchán (楊露禪, 1799–1872) returned to his native village from Chenjiagou, the Yǔxiāng brothers admired his art and studied the old-frame Chen family taijiquan with him, grasping its essentials. In 1852 Yǔxiāng's elder brother Chéngqīng (澄清, 1800–1884) passed the metropolitan civil-service examination and took office as magistrate of Wuyang county in Henan. Yǔxiāng travelled to his brother's post and, taking the route through Wen county, stopped at Chenjiagou intending to seek further instruction from Lùchán's teacher Chén Chángxīng (陳長興, 1771–1853). It happened that Chángxīng was already old and ill — he was then 82 and died the following year — and at the time Chén Qīngpíng (陳青萍) was teaching the new-frame Chen taijiquan at Zhaobao town; Yǔxiāng therefore studied the new frame with Qīngpíng for over a month, and came to know its theory and method thoroughly. At a salt shop in Wuyang, Chéngqīng obtained Wáng Zōngyuè's (王宗岳, a man of the Qianlong reign) Taijiquan Manual. Yǔxiāng studied the manual and gained further insight; drawing on his own experience of practising, he expounded the sense of Wang's old text, writing ten annotations to the Thirteen Postures Practice Song, under the title Essential Words on Pushing Words; he further expanded this into the Thirteen Postures Practice Heart-Explanation in four sections, and distilled the essentials of training into the Ten Requirements of Body Method. All his writings rest on his own experience, and are therefore concise and to the point, without a single ornamental phrase.

Yǔxiāng's nephew Lǐ Jīnglún (李經綸), courtesy name Yìshē (亦畬, 1832–1892), began studying with Yǔxiāng in 1853. In his own study of taijiquan Yìshē imitated Yǔxiāng's method of practical testing — summoning village braves to test his own skill — and revised his notes again and again. Yìshē's grandson Huáiyīn (槐蔭) wrote in the 1935 Li Lianrang-hall edition Preface to the Taijiquan Manual: "This manual was written by my grandfather in his later years, and was completed only after many revisions. Whenever he found a posture ingenious or a key point of a technique, he would write it on a slip of paper and paste it at his seat, testing and pondering it. After some days, if he felt something was not right and ought to be changed, he would tear it down and substitute another slip. This pasting and tearing went back and forth until it was perfect and only then stopped; in time the book was complete." Lǐ Yìshē's taijiquan essays comprise the Five-Character Formula; the Secret Formula of Release, with its four seven-character lines on bracing, drawing, reaching and releasing; and Essential Words for Practice in Walking the Frame and Pushing Hands, which sets out that walking the frame is pushing hands and pushing hands is walking the frame, and that form practice and pushing hands complete each other. In the spread of taijiquan in modern times, the greater part of the credit for ceaseless innovation in teaching materials and methods goes to three generations of the Yang family; but in the study and summation of the art's theory, the first place goes to Wu and Li. Compared with the abstract generality of Wang Zongyue's Taijiquan Treatise, theirs is far more concrete and practical — they inherit and they develop, and so were able to form a school of their own.

The Wu Yuxiang form differs both from the old and new Chen frames and from the Yang large and small frames; it studies them and transforms them, forming a school of its own. Its characteristics are: a compact posture; unhurried movement; a footwork method that strictly distinguishes substantial from insubstantial; and a chest and abdomen that, in advancing, retreating and rotating rotating, always keep upright and centred. The outer form is governed entirely by the inner conversion of substantial and insubstantial and by a "hidden turning of inner qi"; each hand governs half the body, without overstepping, and the hand does not extend past the toe. There were originally leaping movements as well, but by the fourth transmission, with Hǎo Yuèrú (郝月如, 1877–1935), these were changed to no jumping and no leaping; the double lotus-swing was likewise changed so that the foot is not slapped — reforms made to suit the needs of the old and the physically weak.

In Wu-style pushing hands the footwork is of one kind only: three and a half steps forward and three and a half steps back.

Yǔxiāng taught and read for his own pleasure, and Yìshē practised medicine for a living; both held themselves to be scholars, and accepted very few pupils in their home district. Among those Li transmitted it to, the most refined in skill was his fellow-townsman Hǎo Hé (郝和), courtesy name Wéizhēn (為真, 1849–1920). When Wu-style taijiquan first came to Beijing in the early Republic, some called it the Li frame; and when Wéizhēn's son Yuèrú and his grandson Shàorú went together to Nanjing and Shanghai in 1928 to teach, some called it the Hao frame. Yuèrú left behind several writings on taijiquan. Among the later generations of Wu and Li, few devoted themselves to taijiquan, and so Wu-style was passed on and practised by the Hao family. Shàorú had not taught for many years, until the Shanghai Municipal Gymnasium, in order to recover the tradition, opened a Wu-style taijiquan class in 1961 and invited Hǎo Shàorú to teach, so that it might be spread more widely. Wu-style taijiquan had never before appeared in illustrated form; the posture plates in this book were photographed from Hǎo Shàorú, who also wrote the movement explanations.

(Gù Liúxīn)

Foreword

Foreword

I grew up in a family with a passion for taijiquan. Our native place is Yongnian county in Hebei. My grandfather's name was Hé, his courtesy name Wéizhēn. My father's name was Wénguì, his courtesy name Yuèrú. My grandfather learned taijiquan from our neighbour Mr Lǐ Yìshē (see note 1). Mr Li had been taught it by his maternal uncle, Mr Wǔ Yǔxiāng (see note 2). My grandfather studied with Mr Li for a long time, never interrupting for decades, right up to the time of Mr Li's death. Thus my grandfather came to possess deeply the subtleties of the Wu–Li school of taijiquan. While my grandfather was practising with Mr Li, my father was often at his side, because he was a pupil in Mr Li's traditional school. Through long observation and pondering, my father could already grasp the theory and practice of taijiquan before he came of age. From childhood I too, because of my surroundings, came early into contact with taijiquan. Afterwards, under my father's instruction, my love for it grew day by day, and by the age of fifteen I had formed the habit of practising every day.

Since Liberation, under the leadership and care of the Party, taijiquan has become a vigorous mass sport and one of the means of health care for the broad masses of the people. This has made me feel deeply that I ought to make public to the whole country the important taijiquan documents preserved in my family and the experience of three generations of my family in teaching taijiquan, and to take an active part in taijiquan research — so as to contribute my small strength to raising and developing this exceedingly precious part of our national cultural heritage. It was from this motive that I wrote this book in my spare time. In the course of writing it, Comrade Gù Liúxīn, director of the Shanghai Municipal Gymnasium, gave me powerful support: he not only read through the whole manuscript himself, but also selected a number of old martial manuals to append at the end of the book, and wrote the short account of Wu-style taijiquan. Comrade Zhōu Yuánlóng drew the diagrams on my behalf, and gave great assistance in the polishing of the text and in the arrangement. In addition, no few friends who love taijiquan took a great part in the work of compilation. Here I express my heartfelt thanks.

Because my own level is limited, shortcomings can hardly be avoided; I hope readers will not begrudge their corrections and criticism.

Chapter One — The Essentials of Taijiquan

Chapter One — The Essentials of Taijiquan
Key Points of Wu-Style Taijiquan

by Hǎo Yuèrú (1) The body methods of taijiquan are chiefly these thirteen: enclosing the chest, drawing up the back, wrapping the hips, guarding the ribs, lifting the crown, suspending the crotch, loosening the shoulders, sinking the elbows, soaring and shifting, flashing and battling, the tailbone held central and upright, qi sunk to the elixir field, and a clear distinction of substantial and insubstantial.

1. What is above the heart is the chest. The chest must not be puffed out; it must relax downward, with the two shoulders drawing slightly forward and together. This is called enclosing the chest (涵胸). Only when the chest can enclose can one move qi by the mind.

2. At the spine between the two shoulders there should be a sense of swelling up; the two shoulders must be lively. One must not bow the head. This is called drawing up the back (拔背).

3. The two knees exert force, with an intent of turning inward, so that the two legs are as one leg, and yet can divide substantial from insubstantial. This is called wrapping the hips (裹胯).

4. The two flanks draw in slightly, taking the posture of gathering below and closing in front, with a feeling of looseness and ease within. This is called guarding the ribs (護肫).

5. Head and neck are straight and correct, neither low nor raised; the spirit threads through to the crown, lifting up the whole body. This is called lifting the crown (提頂).

6. The two thighs exert force, the buttocks push forward, and the lower abdomen has a sense of turning upward. This is called suspending the crotch (吊襠).

7. By intent, loosen the two shoulders and let the qi sink down, adding the single word "stillness" within the intent. This is called loosening the shoulders (鬆肩).

8. Carry qi by intent so that it moves through the two elbows; the wrists must be able to stay lively, and the tips of the elbows should always have a sense of hanging downward. This is called sinking the elbows (沉肘).

9. To have the intent to move but not yet to have moved — that is, the posture of preparation to move — is called soaring and shifting (騰挪).

10. Body, hands, waist and legs follow one another in accord, completing in a single breath and issuing outward, the jin like a loosed arrow, swift as thunder, invincible wherever it goes. This is called flashing and battling (閃戰).

11. The two thighs are powerful, the buttocks draw forward, and the root of the spine lifts the elixir field (the lower abdomen) forward. This is called the tailbone held central and upright (尾閭正中).

12. If one can achieve the tailbone held central and upright, enclosing the chest, guarding the ribs, loosening the shoulders and suspending the crotch, then one can send qi by intent to reach the abdomen without letting it float upward. This is called qi sunk to the elixir field (氣沉丹田).

13. The substantial and insubstantial of the two legs must be clearly distinguished. The insubstantial is not entirely without force: the point where the foot is placed and made substantial must have the posture of soaring and shifting. Soaring and shifting means that foot and chest have the sense of drawing and binding to each other; otherwise one becomes sunk to one side. The substantial is not entirely dead-weighted: the spirit threads through the substantial thigh, which props up the whole body, and there must be a sense of lifting upward. If substantial and insubstantial are not distinguished, one falls into double weighting (雙重).

(2) Hand, eye, body, step, essence, qi, spirit The method of the hand requires that the momentum of the posture soar and shift, that there be a posture of preparation to move, and no sense of looseness or sprawl. The two shoulders must also be loosened, without the least force in them. Hand positions are in themselves not fixed: whether raised or lowered, extended or bent back, there must always be a corresponding intent — when the intent moves, the hand arrives. This is what is meant by "the hand responds as the mind directs". The posture of soaring and shifting is precisely "having intent", "carrying qi", and "concentrating the spirit". Carry qi by intent: in time one becomes refined; refined and more refined, one becomes spiritual; spiritual and more spiritual, one becomes numinous. Whoever grasps this principle will possess the wonder of spiritual clarity. The spirit gathers in the eyes; the eye is the sprout of the heart-mind. Intent is born in the heart: wherever my intent wishes to go, there the gaze shoots straight, and the whole body also faces straight there. When the eye turns, the whole body turns. Regard stillness as movement and movement as stillness — always it must come from the gathering of the spirit.

In the body method, first seek "the tailbone held central and upright". Central and upright means "the root of the spine pointing forward". One must also guard the ribs: if the ribs are not guarded, the tail cannot stand firm, and the whole body has no governing principle. Wherever my intent wishes to go, there the "root of the spine" faces straight. The turning takes place in the two eye-of-the-waist points: turning left, the left waist-eye draws slightly upward and the right waist-eye lifts up the left; turning right, the right waist-eye draws slightly upward and the left waist-eye lifts up the right — and then the tailbone is naturally central and upright. In sum, each item of the body method must be matched one by one; joined together they are a single body method. If one place does not accord, the whole body goes awry — and so the body method may never be wrong. Though there be a thousand changes and ten thousand transformations, one can never step outside this body method.

As for the footwork distinction of substantial and insubstantial: the insubstantial is not entirely without force — within it there must be soaring and shifting; the substantial is not entirely dead-weighted — the spirit must be concentrated through it. Soaring and shifting is called the insubstantial, and within the insubstantial there is the substantial; the spirit is called the substantial, and within the substantial there is the insubstantial. Insubstantial within substantial and substantial within insubstantial, substantial within insubstantial and insubstantial within substantial — this is the meaning.

(3) Rise, carry, open, close In walking the taijiquan frame, each posture is divided into four movements. The first movement is rise (起) — as in the first movement of "left lazy tucking the robe". The second is carry (承) — as in the second movement of that posture. The third is open (開), which is also issuing — as in the third movement. The fourth is close (合), which is also gathering; gathering means storing — as in the fourth movement. But it is not rigid: there is opening within closing and closing upon closing. This is what is meant by neither losing nor resisting, every point exactly matching.

(4) Folding and conversion Taijiquan has the art of folding and the method of conversion. Folding is mutual correspondence: where there is up there is down; where there is forward there is back; where there is left there is right. If the intent is to go up, then lodge a downward intent within it; if the intent is to go down, then lodge an upward intent within it. Forward and back, left and right, are all like this. This is what is called folding. Conversion means the step changes with the body; the source of the commanding intent lies between the waist-eyes. Converting to the left, the left waist-eye draws slightly upward and the right waist-eye lifts up the left; converting to the right, the right waist-eye draws slightly upward and the left waist-eye lifts up the right. This is what is meant by "the source of the commanding intent lies in the waist crevice".

(5) Yielding self to follow others Taijiquan has the art of yielding self to follow others: wherever one is touched, there one is lively. If touched at the hand, the wrist is lively; if touched at the elbow, the elbow can be lively; if touched at the chest, the chest can be lively — and everywhere on the body likewise. Again, touched at the hand, the intent is at the elbow; touched at the elbow, the intent is at the shoulder; touched at the shoulder, the intent is at the chest; touched at the chest, the intent is at the waist; touched at the waist, the intent is at the thigh. Extend this: adhere, connect, stick and follow; neither lose nor resist; draw in and make empty; borrow force to send the other out — all are this same meaning.

Walking the Frame and Pushing Hands in Taijiquan

Walking the Frame and Pushing Hands in Taijiquan

by Hǎo Yuèrú Taijiquan lies not in the form but in the momentum; not in the outer but in the inner. In daily practice of walking the frame, one must study and ponder the way of emptiness, looseness, roundness and liveliness; the spirit and qi must drum and surge; the whole body is like a balloon; the momentum values soaring and shifting; the body is as if suspended in the air. The two hands, whether high or low, bent or extended, one forward one back, one left one right, can all be lively and free. The two legs, whether advancing or retreating, turning left or right, changing substantial and insubstantial, all follow the wish of the intent. With time and deep practice, one reaches the state of not knowing that the hands dance and the feet stamp. When one understands the principle, practises the body method until familiar, is good at using intent and skilful at carrying qi, then at this stage every single movement accords with measure — there is no such thing as wrong.

The student of taijiquan must first seek the tailbone held central and upright. Central and upright means the root of the spine facing between the eyes. Stepping to the left, the left hip draws slightly upward to the left and the right hip lifts up the left; stepping to the right, the right hip draws slightly upward to the right and the left hip lifts up the right — and then the tailbone is naturally central and upright. Being central and upright, one can prop up in all eight directions; propping up in all eight directions, one can rotate freely, and there is nothing to be gained. Next, the footwork's substantial and insubstantial must be clearly distinguished. The insubstantial is not entirely without force — within it there must be soaring and shifting, that is, the posture of preparation to move. The substantial is not entirely dead-weighted — within it the spirit must be concentrated through, that is, the sense of lifting upward. Bear firmly in mind that when the two feet are in front-bow and rear-press, they must not be entirely dead-weighted: one must be insubstantial and one substantial, otherwise one falls into the fault of double weighting. The two shoulders must be loosened, without the least force: with force one cannot yield self to follow others and draw in to make empty. Sinking the elbows means the tips of the elbows always have a downward-sinking intent. The forearms and the two thighs must have within them the posture of soaring and shifting; without soaring and shifting they are not lively, and without liveliness there is no delight of roundness and life. One must also guard the ribs: if the ribs are not guarded, the tail cannot stand firm, and the whole body has no governing principle. One must also nourish qi: qi is nourished straight and without harm — that is, sinking into the elixir field and cultivating it without injury. One must also store jin: jin is stored by bending, with surplus, and must be gathered and concealed within the spine. Inhaling is closing and storing; exhaling is opening and issuing. For in inhaling one can naturally lift up — and can lift another up too; in exhaling one can naturally sink down — and can send another out. This is carrying qi by intent, not compelling qi by force, and it is the way of breathing in taijiquan. (The "breathing" spoken of here refers specifically to the taijiquan "open, close, store, issue" and differs from our ordinary breathing; readers should not mistake it.)

The art of taijiquan is exceedingly subtle and ingenious; it does not rely on great strength or quick hands. Great strength and quick hands are bestowed by nature at birth — what need is there to learn them? Therefore one who wishes to learn this art should first seek it through enclosing the chest, drawing up the back, wrapping the hips, guarding the ribs, lifting the crown, suspending the crotch, loosening the shoulders, sinking the elbows and clearly distinguishing substantial and insubstantial. When these are right, then seek to gather qi: qi is gathered in the spine and pours into the waist. Then seek soaring and shifting. Soaring and shifting is essence, qi and spirit. Essence, qi and spirit are concentrated through the two feet, the two legs, the two hands and the front sections of the two forearms. Wherever the other touches me, there I direct my attention; on the whole body there is not one inch without essence, qi and spirit, not one inch that is not taiji. Only then seek the method of advancing, retreating and rotating. The pivot of rotation lies in the waist crevice. When one can rotate freely without the least disorder, then seek the art of movement and stillness: stillness is nothingness, and from nothingness is born being — that is, intent. Intent has no fixed direction; it must prop up in all eight directions. In solo practice, each posture divides into four words — rise, carry, open, close. After each word, ask: can I prop up in all eight directions? If I cannot prop up in all eight directions, then ponder it at once. If two people In pushing hands my intent must come first. Though the other's hand be quick, it is no match for my intent coming first; though the other's strength be great, it is no match for my qi gathered in. If he strikes with immense force, I meet it with intent, touching only the skin or hair without letting it land, borrowing his force and taking advantage of his momentum — whichever of the eight directions is favourable, there I strike. Bear firmly in mind: do not use force, do not prize breath-force, do not resist, do not lose. One must follow the other and yet still be led by oneself; only having gained the moment and the momentum can one act effectively at will. Movement likewise is intent: the step moves but the body method is not disordered; the hand moves but the momentum is not scattered. In solo practice, at every movement ask: can I convert in all eight directions out of the movement? If I cannot convert in all eight directions, then ponder it at once. In two-person pushing hands, if I wish to go to him, first arrange the whole body well, keep the intent still ahead, fix upon his key point, and go straight at it. The moment my intent touches his skin or hair, if it can respond, one exhale and out he goes. If his force comes resisting and I do not let his force be issued, my intent still borrows his force — neither losing nor resisting — and strikes along his force. This is the marvel of borrowing force to strike, of four ounces deflecting a thousand catties. All of this is carrying qi by intent, not compelling qi by force. When one can strike by intent, in time even intent is not used, and the body method has nothing that does not accord. At this stage one has reached the realm of round harmony and subtle excellence. Say it is, and it is; say it is not, and it is not — every single movement follows the heart's desire. Truly one no longer knows that the hands dance and the feet stamp.

The student of taijiquan must comprehend the principle of taiji. To know the principle of taiji, one must first raise the whole spirit in practice, outwardly showing ease and composure, inwardly consolidating the spirit, with the momentum soaring and shifting and the abdomen drumming and surging. Taiji is the whole body, and the whole body is taiji. Like a balloon: advancing it does not bulge, retreating it does not cave, turning left it is not deficient, turning right it does not collapse — myriad changes, with no interruption whatsoever, completed in a single breath, without outer or inner, form and spirit both forgotten. Only then can one advance into the subtle and refined.

In pushing hands, my intent must be first. Wherever his force touches me, there my intent is employed. The moment his force touches my skin or hair, my intent has already entered his bones. I meet his force with my intent, not with my force colliding against his; exactly neither after nor before, my intent joins with his force. If the left is heavy, then the left is made empty; if the right is heavy, then the right is made void. Raise it and it grows higher; lower it and it grows deeper; advance it and it grows longer; retreat and it grows more pressing. Not a feather can be added, not a fly or insect can alight. Others do not know me, I alone know others. This is what is meant by adhering, connecting, sticking and following; neither losing nor resisting. The student of taijiquan must comprehend the meaning of yin and yang completing each other. In movement they divide; in stillness they join. Dividing is opening wide; joining is contracting small — and in both, the two qi of yin and yang open and close, convert, and answer each other, never parting from start to finish. Opening is largeness, not colliding; contracting is smallness, not dodging. In one movement there is no part that does not move; in one stillness there is no part that is not still. Movement is the turning of qi; stillness is the posture of preparation to move — this is what is meant by regarding stillness as movement and movement as stillness. Qi is like a cartwheel, the waist like the axle. It is not a matter of the two hands flailing and the body shifting about at random. The crux lies entirely in storing jin: storing jin is like drawing a bow, issuing jin like loosing an arrow. Without storing jin there is no force to loose the arrow. In issuing jin, upper and lower must follow one another; jin rises from the heel of the foot, pours into the waist, and takes form at the fingers. From foot to leg to waist, it must always be one whole qi. The waist is like the grip of the bow, the feet and hands like its tips; within there must be elasticity, and only then is there force to loose the arrow. Arrange oneself well: the moment he touches my skin or hair, my intent fixes upon his jin. To touch skin or hair is neither to lose nor to resist; to meet it with intent is the posture of following along. Being able to follow along, one can borrow force; being able to borrow force, one can strike — this is what is meant by borrowing force to strike, four ounces deflecting a thousand catties. At this stage the hand has measure: it can weigh the size of his jin, can gauge the length of his advance, without the least error. Advancing and retreating, looking left and right, everywhere exactly matching — this is what is meant by "know yourself and know the other, and in a hundred battles you will win a hundred". In daily walking of the frame and pushing hands, one must proceed from this: walking the frame is pushing hands, and pushing hands is walking the frame — the same principle throughout. In walking the frame, each posture must divide into four words: rise, carry, open, close. After each word ask: is it right? If slightly not right, change it at once. A hair's difference loses a thousand li. One who can grasp this meaning will find that walking, standing, sitting and lying are all taiji; the student must not fail to examine this minutely.

In daily practice of walking the frame, one must by intent sink the qi down and deliver it to the elixir field (by intent, not by force; not by straining qi, not by using breath), keeping and nourishing it, not letting it float up, with the abdomen loose and still and the momentum soaring. Practising according to the method, in time one can naturally gather qi into the bone (the spine). Then, by intent, turn the qi of the spine upward from the tailbone through the elixir field. Reaching this stage, one can carry qi by intent throughout the whole body. Wherever he touches me, there my intent arrives, and the qi likewise comes out after it, as echo answers sound, swift as lightning. There is no place on the whole body where it is not so. This is what is meant by "carrying qi as through nine winding pearls, reaching the smallest place; issuing jin like steel refined a hundred times, nothing so hard it cannot break" — and likewise "where intent arrives, qi arrives". Moreover the qi of the elixir field must be nourished straight and without harm, and only then can it be like the water of the long river and the great sea, inexhaustible in use, unfailing in supply. When the practice is mature and one has refined the whole body into a single family, just like a balloon — left heavy then left empty, right heavy then right void — things come and one responds, and nothing fails to match. All of this is "carrying qi by intent", not "compelling qi by force", "within and not without" — that is, "he who prizes breath-force has no force; he who nourishes qi is purely firm".

Some Reflections on Teaching and Practice

Hǎo Shàorú Drawing on more than thirty years of practising and teaching, I hold that whether teaching or practising, one must first of all fix one's eyes on the body method — the eight body methods of Wǔ Yǔxiāng — and must proceed from the inner outward.

The body method is one of the principal contents of taijiquan theory. In both teaching and practice, the body method is at once the most basic and, from first to last, the most important. Therefore the body method must be required strictly.

In practising taijiquan, to say nothing of reaching refined skill, even basic skill cannot be attained in a single step. Therefore in teaching or practising, one must broadly divide the work into two stages.

The first stage is the practice of the outer form: that is, learning the frame, with attention to the body method. But the eight body methods cannot all be grasped at once. One must first choose one or two as the emphasis emphasis, and after these are mastered, gradually add more. The coordination of the upper and lower limbs with the body method is also extremely important, because poor coordination will directly affect the body method. Coordination of the upper limbs is comparatively easy to master; but the lower limbs, since they must both support the body's stability and attend to distinguishing substantial from insubstantial, are more difficult for beginners. Only when these eight body methods are mastered can the muscles and bones of the whole body be lively and harmonious, with movements unified, and only then can one reach the stage of following the heart's desire.

The second stage is the practice of the inner form, also called inner jin. One must first take the practice of intent as one's guide, and gradually refine intent, qi and frame into one. From outer form to inner form, from coarse qi to refined qi. Then there is no outer and no inner, no coarse and no refined, all in a great oblivion. Only when practice reaches this stage can one climb the high peak of taijiquan art along a road of ceaseless advance. As for those who taste but briefly and stop, or who set up novelties out of thin air, they are not worth taking as models.

Explanation of the Sequence Diagrams

All the movements of Wu-style taijiquan are arranged according to the sequence of rise, carry, open, close. Four movements make up one sequence of rise, carry, open, close. For example, the four movements of the second posture, "left lazy tucking the robe", are the four diagrams in the sequence diagrams: Figure 1 is "rise", Figure 2 is "carry", Figure 3 is "open", Figure 4 is "close" — joined into one sequence. The entire set is arranged as sequences joined one to the next. Between one sequence and the next, though there appears to be a pause, one must keep the jin broken but the intent unbroken, and internally the sequences must still join in an unbroken thread — and so in practice one must not break off because of the sequence divisions.

A sequence is mostly formed from a single posture, as with the "left lazy tucking the robe" cited above, which has four movements and makes one sequence. But there are also cases where two, or even four or five postures combine into one sequence — for example the fourth posture, "single whip", and the fifth, "raise the hand", whose two postures are joined together as four movements into one sequence.

Illustrated explanation of the Wu-style taijiquan form

Illustrated explanation of the Wu-style taijiquan form
First posture — Preparatory posture (Figure 1)

The two feet stand together (about one fist apart), the two knees slightly bent, not stiff; the two arms hang naturally; the eyes look straight ahead. (Figure 1)

The requirement, when the two feet stand together and one has taken up the stance, is that the body be natural and calm; that the whole spirit be raised and distracting thoughts excluded; that the intent contain a lifting energy (ding jin), so that the head is upright and straight, the chin slightly drawn in; that the two shoulders be relaxed; that the abdomen be loosened, with the breath sunk to the dantian; that the chest be contained, the back pulled up, the crotch wrapped, and the ribs protected.

Second posture — Left grasp and tie the garment (Figures 2–5)

Movement one: the two legs bend at the knees and sink slightly; the body weight gradually moves onto the right leg, and the right leg becomes full (solid); the left heel lifts, the toe touches the ground, and the left leg becomes empty. At the same time the two arms bend slightly and the two hands move forward, resting at the sides of the hips, the backs of the hands upward, the fingers pointing forward, slightly flexed and separated. (Figure 2)

Throughout the form of Wu-style taijiquan, the two arms and two legs of every movement and posture may never be straightened, but must keep a certain bend; and when the body turns, the heel of the full leg must serve as the axis. Moreover, in every movement the head, trunk, chest, back and the other parts must accord with the requirements of the preparatory posture; this will not be repeated below.

Movement two: the body turns slightly to the left, and the left foot accordingly lifts and steps out toward the left diagonal (a 45-degree diagonal; the diagonals referred to from here on are all 45 degrees), the toe tilted up and the heel touching the ground, the left leg slightly bent (the two legs are still empty-left and full-right). At the same time the two hands lift toward the chest, the fingers pointing up to form upright palms, the left hand outside, level with the mouth; the right hand inside, level with the heart, both palms turned slightly forward (the two elbows must sink); facing the southeast diagonal. (Figure 3)

Movement three: the right heel presses, the left leg bows forward, the left sole settles flat, the body weight moves onto the left leg, the right leg bends slightly, and the two legs become full-left and empty-right. At the same time the two hands push slowly forward (the upper body may not lean forward or backward; the shoulders must be relaxed and the elbows sunk), the palms still turned slightly forward, the left hand level with the eyes, the right hand level with the chest. (Figure 4)

Movement four: the two hands fall at the same time; when the two hands come level with the hips (with the two hands down they may not let the elbows stand out behind the back) they part to left and right, arc upward, and the upright palms come together before the chest with the intent of holding a ball, the two hands level with the mouth, the palms turned slightly forward. At the same time as the two hands part left and right, the right foot follows forward to beside the left heel, touching the ground with the toe. (Figure 5)

The requirement is to relax the shoulders and sink the elbows; there should be a lively roundness between the hands and the chest. The two legs are still full-left and empty-right.

Third posture — Right grasp and tie the garment (Figures 6–9)

Movement one: with the left heel as the axis (the right toe still touching the ground), the body turns slightly to the right, facing due south, the two legs still full-left and empty-right. (Figure 6)

Movement two: the waist revolves slightly to the right, and the right foot accordingly steps out toward the right diagonal, the toe tilted up and the heel touching the ground, the right leg slightly bent (the two legs still full-left and empty-right). At the same time the left hand moves from above downward and the right hand from below upward, drawing a small circle, still as upright palms (fingers pointing up): the right hand outside, level with the mouth; the left hand inside, level with the heart; both palms turned slightly forward (the two elbows must sink); facing the southwest diagonal. (Figure 7)

Movements three and four are the same as movements three and four of the second posture, "Left grasp and tie the garment", only that left and right are reversed and the direction is different. The direction of this posture is toward the southwest diagonal. (Figures 8– 9)

Fourth posture — Single whip (Figures 10–12)
Fifth posture — Raise the hand and lift the posture (Figure 13)

The two postures are joined into one section-sequence (as to what a section-sequence is, see the explanation of the section-sequence diagrams). Movements one, two and three are "Single whip"; movement four is "Raise the hand and lift the posture".

Movement one: with the right heel as the axis (the left toe still touching the ground), the body turns 90 degrees to the left, facing the southeast diagonal, still with the right leg full and the left leg empty. (Figure 10) Movement two: the left foot steps out, the toe tilted up and the heel touching the ground, the two legs still empty-left and full-right. (Figure 11)

Movement three: the right heel presses, the left leg bows forward, the left sole settles flat, the right leg bends slightly, and the two legs become full-left and empty-right. At the same time the two hands part to left and right, the fingers pointing up, the palms turned outward, still as upright palms: the left hand level with the eyes, the right hand level with the mouth (the two elbows must sink); facing the diagonal east, slightly toward south. (Figure 12)

At the same time the body turns slightly to the right, facing due south. The two hands follow the movement: the right hand arcs downward and to the left, scooping up from below and coming to rest before the abdomen, the fingers pointing forward; the left hand rises, the palm turned up, with the intent of lifting upward, level with the eyebrows (the shoulders must relax and the elbows sink). (Figure 13)

Sixth posture — The white goose spreads its wings (Figures 14–17)

Movement one: the right toe still touching the ground, with the left heel as the axis, the body turns slightly to the right, facing the southwest diagonal, the two legs still full-left and empty-right. (Figure 14)

Movement two: the right foot steps forward, the toe tilted up and the heel touching the ground, the right leg slightly bent (the two legs still full-left and empty-right). At the same time the right hand lifts from the outside, the palm held crosswise and raised to above the forehead, the palm turned forward; the left hand comes down from the inside, passing before the face as an upright palm, and comes level with the heart (the shoulders must relax and the elbows sink). (Figure 15)

Movement three: the left heel presses, the right leg bows forward, the right sole settles flat, and the two legs become full-right and empty-left. At the same time the left hand pushes slowly forward (the upper body may not lean forward or backward), the left hand level with the chest and the palm turned slightly forward (the right hand must have the intent of turning upward; the shoulders must relax and the elbows sink). (Figure 16)

Movement four: the right hand extends slightly forward and the elbow hangs down; the two hands fall at the same time; when the two hands come level with the hips (as the two hands fall, the elbows may not stand out behind the back) they part to left and right, arc upward, and the upright palms come together before the chest with the intent of holding a ball, the two hands level with the mouth and the palms turned slightly forward. At the same time as the two hands part left and right, the left foot follows forward to beside the right heel, touching the ground with the toe; facing the right-front diagonal. (Figure 17)

The requirement is that there be a lively roundness between the hands and the chest; the two legs are still full-right and empty-left.

In practising taijiquan, every movement requires relaxed shoulders and sunk elbows throughout; the reader is asked to bear this firmly in mind, and it will not be repeated below.

Seventh posture — Brush the knee and twist the step (Figures 18–21)

Movement one: the left toe shifts slightly and touches the ground behind to the left; with the right heel as the axis, the body turns 135 degrees to the left, facing due east, the two legs still full-right and empty-left. (Figure 18)

Movement two: the body turns slightly to the left, facing the northeast diagonal. The hands and feet follow the movement: the left foot steps out toward the left diagonal, the toe tilted up and the heel touching the ground, the left leg slightly bent, the two legs still full-right and empty-left; the left hand falls to before the abdomen; the right hand rises beside the right cheek, level with the ear, the fingers pointing up and the palm turned forward; facing the northeast diagonal. (Figure 19)

Movement three: the right heel presses, the left leg bows forward, the left sole settles flat, the right leg bends slightly, and the two legs become full-left and empty-right. At the same time the left hand brushes downward past the left knee and comes to rest beside the left leg, the fingers pointing forward; the right hand pushes slowly forward (the upper body may not lean forward or backward), the fingers still pointing up, level with the eyes; the face still toward the northeast diagonal. (Figure 20)

Movement four: the right hand falls past the side of the left leg (fingers pointing forward); when it comes level with the left hand, the two hands part to left and right and arc upward, and the upright palms come together before the chest with the intent of holding a ball, the two hands level with the mouth, the palms turned slightly forward; at the same as the two hands part left and right, the right foot in front follows to beside the left heel, touching the ground with the toe, and the two legs are still full-left and empty-right. (Figure 21)

Illustrated explanation of Wu-style taijiquan push-hands Taijiquan push-hands was originally called "hitting hands". Of what Wu Yuxiang and Li Yishe handed down, there is only the one kind — moving-step push-hands. Why adopt only moving-step push-hands and not fixed-step? The reason is that moving-step push-hands can be applied with agility and has no defect of stiffness; it trains both the stepping method and the body method, and at the same time allows practice of "stick, connect, adhere, follow". Moreover, in the course of the movement one is constantly able to keep a centred and stable posture, and can still issue energy while in motion. Once practised to familiarity, one can reach what the ancients called "walking, stopping, sitting, lying" — all of them states of practising the form.

The stepping method of Wu-style push-hands is three and a half steps forward, three and a half steps back. On the first and second steps forward, the advancing foot is placed on the outside of the opponent's front foot; on the third step forward, one foot is placed between the opponent's steps; on the fourth step the rear foot moves up beside the front foot with the toe touching the ground lightly, which is called a half-step. Whether placed on the outside of the opponent or between his steps, one should keep slightly close to the opponent's front foot. On one straight line I advance and he retreats, and he advances and I retreat. Above, the four hands of "ward-off, roll-back, press, push" are applied, the same as in the other kinds of push-hands, so they will not be drawn and explained again; only a few of the postures in which energy is issued are set out below for reference. Treatise on taijiquan

Wang Zongyue of Shanyou Taiji is born of wuji and is the mother of yin and yang. In movement it divides; in stillness it joins. There are very many side doors to this art; though the postures differ, they amount to no more than the strong cheating the weak and the slow giving way to the quick. Still as a mountain, moving as a river. Step as if on the brink of an abyss, move power as if drawing silk, store power as if drawing a bow, issue power as if loosing an arrow. Move the breath like a nine-bend pearl, so that no subtlety is unreached; move power like steel tempered a hundred times, and what hardness will it not shatter. In form like a falcon seizing a hare, in spirit like a cat catching a mouse. Seek the straight within the curved, store and then issue. Gathering is issuing, continuous without break. Utterly soft, and only then can one be utterly firm; able to stick and lean, and only then can one be agile. Nourish the breath by what is straight and it does no harm; store the power by what is curved and it has surplus. Gradually one comes to respond to things as they come — this too is "knowing to stop, and so attaining."

Again it is said:

First in the mind, afterwards in the body; belly loose, breath gathered into the bones, spirit at ease, body still, moment by moment keeping the mind. Remember always: when one thing moves, nothing is unmoved; when one thing is still, nothing is unstilled. Regard stillness as movement, regard movement as stillness. In moving, pulling to and fro, the breath clings to the back, gathered into the spine — be still. Inwardly solidify the spirit, outwardly show ease and comfort. Step as a cat walks, move power as if drawing silk. The whole body's intent lies in storing spirit, not in breath; if in breath, then it stagnates. He who has breath has no power; he who has no breath is purely firm. The breath is like a cartwheel, the waist like the axle.

Again it is said:

When the other does not move, I do not move; when the other moves slightly, I move first. As if loose yet not loose, about to unfold yet not — unfold. The power breaks but the intent does not break.

Again it is said:

In every movement, the hand alone first applies force, and then immediately loosens. One must still thread it through, and this is nothing other than beginning, sustaining, turning and closing. First the intent moves, then the power moves; the transition must be strung on a single thread. The breath should be stirred and agitated, the spirit should be gathered inward. Do not allow any place of defect, do not allow any place of concavity or convexity, do not allow any place of break or interruption. Its root is in the feet, it issues from the legs, it is governed by the waist, it takes form in the fingers. From feet to legs to waist, it must always be one complete breath. Advancing and retreating, only then does one gain the opportunity and the position; where one does not gain opportunity and position, the body becomes scattered, and one must come to lean and tilt — the fault must be sought in the waist and legs, and it is so above and below, before and behind, left and right. All of this is intent, not outward form: where there is above there is below, where there is front there is back, where there is left there is right. If the intent wishes to go up, then lodge the intent below; if a thing is about to be lifted, and you add a breaking force to it, then its root breaks of itself, and it is destroyed swiftly, beyond doubt. Fullness and emptiness should be clearly distinguished: each place has its own fullness and emptiness, and every place is always this one fullness and emptiness; the whole body is threaded joint by joint — do not let there be the least interruption.

Releasing in pushing hands

Five-word formula　Li Yiyu
One: the mind is still

If the mind is not still, then it is not focused; as soon as one raises a hand, front, back, left and right have no fixed direction at all — hence the mind must be still. At first the movements cannot come from oneself; one must calm the mind and personally recognise it, following what the other does, yielding and extending with him, neither losing nor resisting, not extending and contracting on one's own. If he has force, I too have force, and my force is first; if he has no force, I — also have no force, and my intent is still first. One must keep the mind on it moment by moment: wherever one is touched, the mind must be applied there; one must seek the news within "neither losing nor resisting." Working on in this way, in a year or half a year one can apply it in the body. This is entirely intent, not the use of force; in time, others are controlled by me, and I am not controlled by others.

Two: the body is agile

If the body is stagnant, then one cannot advance and retreat freely; hence the body must be agile. In raising a hand one must not have a stupid look. When the other's force has just touched my skin and hair, my intent has already entered his bones; both hands brace, and one breath runs through. When the left is heavy, the left empties and the right has already gone; when the right is heavy, the right empties and the left has already gone. The breath is like a cartwheel: the whole body must follow along. Where it does not follow, the body becomes scattered and cannot get force — the fault must be sought in the waist and legs. First use the mind to command the body, following others and not oneself. Afterwards the body can follow the mind, and even proceeding from oneself is still following others. Proceeding from oneself one stagnates; following others one lives. If one can follow others, then in the hands there is measure. Weigh the size of his force, and there is no error of a fraction; gauge the length of his advance, and there is no difference of a hair. Advancing and retreating, everywhere exactly fitting — the longer the work, the finer the skill.

Three: the breath gathers

If the breath's momentum is scattered and loose, then there is no containment, and the body easily scatters; one must cause the breath to gather into the spine. The breathing is numinous and unobstructed, and the whole body has no gap. Inhaling is closing and storing; exhaling is opening and issuing. For inhaling naturally lifts one up, and can also lift the other up; exhaling naturally sinks one down, and can also send the other out. This is moving the breath by intent, not driving the breath by force.

Four: the power is whole

The power of the whole body is trained into one family, distinguishing clearly fullness and emptiness. In issuing power there must be a root: the power rises from the heel, is governed at the waist, takes form in the fingers, and issues from the spine and back. One must also raise up the whole of one's spirit: at the moment when the other's power is about to issue but has not yet issued, my power has already joined his power — exactly neither behind nor before, like skin catching fire, like a spring gushing out. Advancing and retreating, there is not the least scattering; seek the straight within the curved, store and then issue, and only then can one succeed at once with the hand. This is what is meant by "borrowing force to strike the man, four ounces deflecting a thousand catties."

Five: the spirit gathers

When the above four are all present, they all return to the gathering of the spirit. When the spirit gathers, then one breath forges and casts, refining the breath and returning it to the spirit, and the breath's momentum soars and shifts. The spirit is poured in; opening and closing have order; fullness and emptiness are clear. When the left is empty, the right is full; when the right is empty, the left is full. Emptiness is not being utterly without force — the breath's momentum must have soaring and shifting; fullness is not being utterly locked up — the spirit must be valued and poured in. The crux lies entirely in the transforming within the chest and waist, not outside. Force is borrowed from others; breath issues from the spine. How can breath issue from the spine? The breath sinks downward, gathering from both shoulders into the spine and pouring into the waist — this is the breath going from above to below, and it is called closing. From the waist it takes form in the spine, spreads to both arms, and is applied in the fingers — this is the breath going from below to above, and it is called opening. Closing is gathering; opening is issuing. If one can understand opening and closing, then one knows yin and yang. Arriving at this stage, one day's work is one day's refinement of skill, until gradually one follows what the mind desires, and nothing fails to accord with one's intent.

Secret formula for releasing　Li Yiyu

Lift, draw, loosen, release Lift up his body, borrowing his force. Within it is the word "numinous." Draw him to the front of the body, and the power only then stores. Within it is the word "gather." Loosen my power and do not let it bend. Within it is the word "still." In releasing, the waist and feet must recognise the exact point. Within it is the word "whole."

Essential words on practising the form and pushing hands　Li Yiyu

The men of old said: "One can draw in and let fall into emptiness, and can deflect a thousand catties with four ounces; one cannot draw in and let fall into emptiness, and cannot deflect a thousand catties with four ounces." The words are very comprehensive, and the beginner has no way to grasp them. I add a few phrases to explain them, so that those who have the will in this art may find the way in, and so advance daily with achievement. If one wishes to draw in and let fall into emptiness, and to deflect a thousand catties with four ounces, one must first know oneself and know others. If one wishes to know oneself and know others, one must first give up up the self and follow others. If one wishes to give up the self and follow others, one must first gain the opportunity and the position. If one wishes to gain opportunity and position, one must first make the whole body one family. If one wishes to make the whole body one family, one must first have no defect in the whole body. If one wishes to have no defect in the whole body, one must first have the spirit and breath stirred and agitated. If one wishes the spirit and breath stirred and agitated, one must first raise up the spirit, so that the spirit cannot scatter outward. If one wishes the spirit not to scatter outward, one must first have the spirit and breath gather and enter the bones. If one wishes the spirit and breath to gather and enter the bones, one must first have strength in the front section of both thighs. Both shoulders loosen, the breath sinks downward, the power rises from the heel, changes in the legs, is contained in the chest, moves in both shoulders, and is governed in the waist. Above, the two arms are linked; below, the two legs follow. The power is exchanged from within: gathering is closing, releasing is opening. When still, all is still; stillness is closing, and within closing is lodged opening. When moving, all moves; movement is opening, and within opening is lodged closing. Touched, one then turns and revolves freely, and nowhere fails to get force. Only then can one draw in and let fall into emptiness, and deflect a thousand catties with four ounces. In ordinary practice of the form, this is the work of knowing oneself: at each movement, first ask whether one's whole body accords with these several items; if it accords but slightly not, then change at once — hence in practising the form one should be slow and not fast. Pushing hands is the work of knowing others; movement and stillness are indeed knowing others, but it is still asking oneself. If one has arranged oneself well, then when another touches me, I do not move him a hair's breadth, but take advantage of his momentum to enter, join and fix his power, and he falls out of himself. If one has a place where one cannot get force, then it is double-weighting not yet transformed, and one must seek it in the opening and closing of yin and yang. This is what is meant by "know yourself and know others, and in a hundred battles you will win a hundred victories."

Four-word secret formula　(author to be verified)

Four-word secret formula　(author to be verified)

Fu (spread): Fu means moving the breath in one's own body and spreading it over the other's power, so that he cannot move.

Gai (cover): Gai means using the breath to cover the place from which the other comes.

Dui (match): Dui means using the breath to match the place from which the other comes, fixing the aim and then going out.

Tun (swallow): Tun means using the breath to swallow him wholly and enter into transformation.

These four words are without form and without sound; only after understanding power, when one has refined to the most exquisite stage, can one know them — completely it is spoken of in terms of breath. If one can nourish one's breath by what is straight and without harm, only then can one apply it to the four limbs, and the four limbs will speak without words.

Textual note: Li Fuyin, grandson of Li Yiyu, compiled the Lianrang Hall edition of the Taijiquan Manual in 1935 and took the "Four-word secret formula" to be a work of Wu Yuxiang, basing this on the Brief Account of My Late Grandfather, the Honourable Lianquan, written by Wu Laixu, grandson of Wu Yuxiang. Laixu said of his grandfather that "receiving the art, his insights came entirely from the four-word formula," and so Fuyin took the "Four-word secret formula" to be Wu Yuxiang's work. Considering that this formula's boxing theory is lofty and subtle, with a level above Li Yiyu's "Secret formula for releasing"; that Li Yiyu's Taijiquan Manual, written out for Hao He in 1881, fully records Wu Yuxiang's boxing treatises; that Wu Yuxiang died in 1880; and that Wu and Li stood as teacher and student and lived in the same village — if this formula were Wu Yuxiang's work, there is no way it would not have been included. Hence this formula is probably not Wu Yuxiang's work, and may have been composed by a disciple of Wu or of Li. Li Yiyu died in 1892, so it is also possible that he wrote this formula. It is singled out here for further examination.
