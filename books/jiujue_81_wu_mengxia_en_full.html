Publisher's Note

This book contains an annotated edition of the Nine Formulas of Taijiquan and an annotated edition of the practice methods for the Eighty-One Postures. Both the Nine Formulas and the Eighty-One Postures were handed down by the celebrated taijiquan master Yang Banhou. Wu Mengxia demonstrates the movements and annotates them one by one, so that the reader may study and take reference from taijiquan's martial applications.

The book was first published in 1958. At the request of readers it is now reprinted from the original plates, without alteration.

Preface

Preface

The author has loved the martial arts, and takes particular pleasure in boxing methods such as taijiquan, xingyiquan and baguazhang. Thirty years ago I learned taijiquan from my teacher Niu Lianyuan. Master Niu was a leading disciple of the celebrated taijiquan master Yang Banhou, and received the secret transmission of the Nine Formulas of taijiquan from the Yang family. Master Niu passed these nine formulas on to me. I treasured them for many years and would not lightly disclose them to anyone. In 1940, in Kunming, through the introduction of Mr. Jin Yiming I became acquainted with a fellow enthusiast, Mr. Wu Zhiqing. We talked and took to each other at once, and I told him three of the formulas — namely "The Formula of the Thirteen-Character Action", "The Formula of the Eight-Character Method" and "The Formula of Empty and Full". These he afterwards included, in 1943, in his second edition of The Authentic Taijiquan. In that book Mr. Zhiqing praised these formulas, saying: "Each character has its own use, each sentence has its own method; every character is a pearl, every sentence a brocade."

In recent years I have received the education of the Party and the government, and have been deeply moved by the Communist Party's spirit of selflessness and whole-hearted service to the people; this has further aroused my enthusiasm to contribute my small share to the people's work of health care. Accordingly, in the leisure left me by my medical practice in acupuncture and moxibustion, I have compiled this manuscript on the Nine Formulas and Eighty-One Postures of taijiquan: on the one hand, bringing the Nine Formulas secretly transmitted by the Yang family fully into the open, with a simple annotation for the reader's study and reference; and on the other, setting out the practice methods of the Eighty-One Postures in words, for the reader to imitate and practise. This set of eighty-one postures was formerly called the "Great Function Frame"; its postures and movements are largely the same as, with small differences from, the Great Frame handed down by Yang Chengfu. Unfortunately the author's skill is impure, and the demonstrated movements are not as ideal as they might be.

It should be explained that the meaning of the nine formulas is entirely concerned with the applications of martial technique; therefore, in There is no contradiction; on the contrary, through complex methods of martial technique the training value of the martial art is actually enhanced. It must be made clear that our aim in practising taijiquan lies in strengthening the constitution, not in striking people or defending ourselves. The martial technique we speak of is likewise subordinate to this aim. Only the author is limited in his level; the annotation may not be exhaustive, and I respectfully hope that readers will point out its defects and offer their opinions, so that we may study it together.

Wu Mengxia December 1957

The Main Requirements for Practising Taijiquan

The Main Requirements for Practising Taijiquan

The main requirements of taijiquan as a physical exercise are: relaxation (松, sōng), steadiness (稳, wěn), slowness (慢, màn), evenness (匀, yún), together with unbroken continuity (连贯不断) and calm, expansive breathing (平气舒展). Here we shall discuss in particular the four words relaxation, steadiness, slowness and evenness.

Relaxation (松, sōng): both within and without, the organism must be loose and comfortable. First relax the brain — commonly called "calming the heart and settling the breath"; the heart is the intent, and before and after beginning the practice one must cast out all distracting thoughts, concentrating wholly on the meaning of "movement born of stillness", so that the higher nerve centres of the brain are not tense but rest and readjust in a pleasant looseness, giving free play to their power of governing the body's various systems and thereby attaining the effect upon the internal organs.

Steadiness (稳, wěn): in practice the postures must be steady, centred and upright, fulfilling "the tailbone centred and the spirit threading the crown". "The tailbone centred" means holding the spine and cervical vertebrae vertical, and using the moving power at the centre to employ the supporting power of the centre of gravity, avoiding any leaning to one side, so that the whole body obtains balanced activity. "The spirit threading the crown", also called "suspending the head-top", means leading the spirit and vital breath to the crown; it is the expression of the spirit having something to hold to and the intent having something to think upon — that is, gathering up the dispersed power of the higher nerve centres, causing it to concentrate upon a single imagined aim.

Slowness (慢, màn): in practice the movements must be slow, directed by the intent and expressed in the limbs; the movement born of the utmost stillness is the utmost movement, and the speed born of the utmost slowness is the utmost speed. Therefore in practice one must be neither hurried nor rash: within this activity that seems to move yet moves without haste, seems still yet is not purely still, one completes the whole set of practice methods, so that the joints of the whole body obtain a transcendent activity beyond their ordinary range. Yet though the outside moves, it does not disturb the inside. Practice of this kind not only does not harm the qi but can nourish it, and can prevent the sinews, bones, joints and muscles of the whole body from undergoing violent compression. Owing to the rising, falling, turning and folding of the movements, one may gradually attain the goal of a healthy constitution. Hence for the aged and the weak, this bodily training that proceeds from the shallow to the deep is suitable.

Evenness (匀, yún): in the postures of practice, what should be extended should be extended as far as possible, and what should be compact should likewise be compactly and appropriately so; there must be no movements of huddling-up or of stagnant qi, and both the breathing and the postures must be even, uniform and calm. In this way one not only aids the circulation of the blood but can also gradually strengthen the vital capacity of the lungs.

Within this relaxation, steadiness, slowness, evenness and unbroken continuity, one completes the whole set of taijiquan's practice methods, and in time naturally obtains the effect of improving one's state of health.

Chapter One The Essential Formulas of Taijiquan
Section 1 The Original Text of the Nine Essential Formulas of Taijiquan
(1) The Formula of the Great Function of the Whole Body (全体大用诀):

"Infinite are the subtleties of the taijiquan method; From ward-off, roll-back, press and push the sparrow-tail is born. Walking obliquely, single-whip occupies the chest; Turning the body, raise the hand and seal the technique. Scooping the moon from the sea-bottom, the bright wing changes; Flick up and strike the soft ribs, showing no mercy. Brush-the-knee twist-step seeks within the oblique; Hand-plays-the-lute pierces and transforms with fineness. Clinging close to the body, the horizontal elbow rises; Guarding the centre, striking back, one again plays the champion. Advancing, deflection-block intercepts beneath the ribs; Like-seal-like-close guards the centre squarely. The cross-hand method changes without end; Embrace-the-tiger, return-to-the-mountain — pluck and rend complete. Elbow-bottom look-at-fist guards the centre hand; Retreat three steps and reverse the forearm. Sinking the body, retreating, pull and draw back with strength; The oblique-flying technique must not be used in vain. Sea-bottom needle requires bowing the body to reach it; Fan-through-the-back is a lifting and propping work. Twist-body hammer strikes with a flash-transforming form; Crossing the body and advancing, the technique is accomplished. Within the wrist there is in fact a closing and seizing method; Cloud-hands thrice advance to attack upon the arm. High-patl-the-horse intercepts the hand and pierces; Left and right separate-the-foot, the hands must seal. Turn the body and kick with the heel, occupying the belly; Advancing, plant-the-hammer, charging head-on. Reverse the body as the white snake spits its tongue and changes; Pluck the enemy's hand and take his two pupils. Right heel-kick upward at the soft ribs; Left and right drape-the-body, the tiger-taming essence. Above, strike the true chest; below, use at the ribs; Twin-wind-pierces-the-ears, the technique is wondrous. Left heel-kick, kick the right heel form; Turning the body, kick with the heel to meet the knee-bone. Wild-horse-parts-its-mane attacks beneath the armpit; Jade-maid-works-the-shuttles seals the four corners. Shaking and transforming, single arm props the hand up; Left and right, the use is generally the same. Single-whip, lower-the-posture, enter with the edge of the blade; Golden-rooster-stands-on-one-leg takes the upper wind. Lift the knee and strike high at the vital spot; Below, injure the two feet, sparing nothing. The cross-leg method breaks the soft bone; Point-to-the-crotch hammer, leaning down, is the blade. Step-forward-seven-stars, the frame-hand form; Step-back-ride-the-tiger dodges to the centre. Turn the body swing-the-lotus, guard the leg and advance; Bend-the-bow-shoot-the-tiger, flick and strike the chest. Like-seal-like-close, watch and settle; The taiji closing-hand form is complete. The great function of the whole body takes intent as its master; The body relaxed, the qi solid, the spirit must be congealed."

(2) The Formula of the Thirteen-Character Action (十三字行动诀):

1. The thirteen characters: "Ward-off (掤), roll-back (揽), press (挤), push (按), pluck (采), rend (挒), elbow (肘), shoulder (靠), advance (进), retreat (退), look (顾), expect (盼), settle (定)."

2. The oral formula: "With the ward-off hand both arms must roundly prop; Still or moving, empty or full, attack at will. Joining hands, roll-back opens, the press-palm is used; The enemy wishes to answer the technique, but his momentum is hard to bring off. With the push-hand technique he seems to topple; Seize the second hand and do not let it go. If his force is fierce, use the rending hand; Elbow and shoulder, at any moment, act at will. Advance, retreat, reverse, turn aside — walk according to the moment; Why fear that the enemy's art is refined? Meeting the enemy, step up and press close to strike; Guard the three-fronts and look to the seven-stars. If the enemy presses close to strike me, Dodge to the centre and strike within the cross. In the method of the thirteen characters of taiji, Ponder the essential meaning with care, and the wonder will be reborn."

(3) The Formula of the Thirteen-Character Practice (十三字用功诀):

"Meeting a hand, on encountering ward-off do not enter the coil; Sticking and adhering without separation, to gain the technique is hard. To close the ward-off one must use the pluck-and-rend method; If the second hand is secured, he has no time for aid. Push firmly on the four cardinal and four corner directions, then change; Touch the hand and at once occupy the first of the first. Roll-back and press, the two methods, seize the moment to use; Elbow and shoulder attack at the front of the foot. Meeting the moment and gaining the advantage, advance and retreat; The three-fronts and the seven-stars lie between look and expect. The whole body's real strength is settled within the intent; Listening, probing, yielding and transforming are the gate of spirit and qi. Seeing the real but not attacking, one gains no striking hand; What day will one's skill be complete in body and use? Practising without applying according to the body, One may cultivate to the end and still the art will not be fine."

(4) The Formula of the Eight-Character Method (八字法诀):

"Three exchanges, two roll-backs, one press and push; Joining hands, on meeting ward-off do not yield precedence. Within the soft there is the hard, and attack cannot break it; Within the hard with no soft, it is not firm. Evading the man's attack and guard, use pluck and rend; The force lies in startling snap and in walking the spiral. Press the advantage and advance, close to the body with the elbow; Shoulder, forearm and knee strike, with shoulder as the first."

(5) The Formula of Empty and Full (虚实诀):

"Empty and full, full and empty, meet within the spirit; Empty and full, full and empty, the hand performs the work. Practising the boxing without understanding empty and full, One wastes one's effort and in the end achieves nothing. Guard the empty, issue the full — exhaust it in the palm; If the centre is full but one does not issue, the art is hard to refine. Empty and full have their own emptiness and fullness; Solid-solid, empty-empty, and the attack is never in vain."

(6) The Formula of the Chaotic Ring (乱环诀):

"The chaotic-ring technique is the hardest to penetrate; Above and below following and joining, its wonder is endless. Lure the enemy deep into the chaotic ring, And the technique of four ounces moving a thousand catties is accomplished. Hand and foot advance together, seeking horizontally and vertically; The chaotic ring in the palm never falls in vain. If you would know where the method within the ring lies, When issuing and landing the point is right, then success is achieved."

(7) The Formula of Yin and Yang (阴阳诀):

"Few are those who cultivate the yin and yang of taiji; Swallowing and spitting, opening and closing, ask of the hard and soft. Cardinal and corner, gathering and releasing, let the lord walk where he will; Stillness and movement, transformation — why need one worry? The two methods of generation and overcoming are used as occasion serves; Dodging and advancing lie wholly in seeking within movement. How does one tell light from heavy, empty from full? When lightness appears within heaviness, do not linger a moment."

(8) The Formula of the Eighteen Presences (十八在诀):

"Ward-off is in the two arms; roll-back is in the palm; press is on the back of the hand; push attacks at the waist; pluck is in the ten fingers; rend is in the two forearms; the elbow is in bending and using; the shoulder is on shoulder and chest; advancing is in cloud-hands; retreating is in turning the forearm; looking is at the three-fronts; expecting is at the seven-stars; settling is in the gap; the centre is in gaining the horizontal; stagnation is in double-weighting; penetration is in single lightness; the empty is in guarding as it should; the real must surely thrust."

(9) The Formula of the Five-Character Classic (五字经诀):

"Casting aside, enter from the side; dodging and unfolding leave no total gap; shoulder the force of the opponent and transform it; rub and grind to test his power; with reserve, holding and storing force, apply it; sticking and adhering never leave the source; follow advancing and retreating and go; restrain the intent and never let it slack; seize and close off the enemy's blood vessels; pull and draw back, sealing with the momentum; soft does not mean using clumsy force; the ward-off arm must roundly prop; rolling in with round, lively force, break the hard and pierce the enemy's point; shield and cover as the enemy plunges fiercely in; pinch and dot in a vital attack; sink and walk with a pulling, drawing momentum; continue unbroken and never lose the gap; press upon the emergence of his empty and full; spread him open — and at once success is achieved."

Chapter Two An Annotation of the Nine Formulas of Taijiquan

Chapter Two An Annotation of the Nine Formulas of Taijiquan
Section 1 The Formula of the Great Function of the Whole Body

"The Great Function of the Whole Body" sets out the practice method for the entire Great Function Frame of taijiquan, and contains within it both body and use. The "body" is the main substance within the boxing; the "use" is the application of the postures within the boxing. Its substance has thirteen characters — namely ward-off (掤), roll-back (揽), press (挤), push (按), pluck (采), rend (挒), elbow (肘), shoulder (靠), advance (进), retreat (退), look (顾), expect (盼), settle (定) — and their use is the martial-technique method of the whole set of postures in practice, together with the principle of the harmony of body and use. The annotation proceeds line by line as follows:

Infinite are the subtleties of the taijiquan method; from ward-off, roll-back, press and push the sparrow-tail is born. Beyond its function of training the body and strengthening the constitution, taijiquan has its own special subtleties in its methods of both practice and use. Within its opening posture, roll-back-the-sparrow's-tail, there are contained the four movements of ward-off, roll-back, press and push, forming the essence of the thirteen characters of the substance.

Walking obliquely, single-whip occupies the chest; turning the body, raise the hand and seal the technique. "Walking obliquely, single-whip occupies the chest": explained according to the practice method, in the martial sense, if the opponent strikes straight at me with his left hand, I quickly hook and hang his incoming hand with my right hand, and at the same time strike his chest with my left hand — this is a use that combines defence with striking, taking defence as attack. At the same moment as my left hand advances, my left leg must drive straight in toward the opponent's crotch; that is, the left hand and left leg must attack together, and the opponent will of himself be struck and sent falling out (the use on left and right is the same).

"Turning the body, raise the hand and seal the technique" is used in the joining-hands method when the two parties come into contact. For example, when the left arm takes the raise-the-hand posture, the left hand must firmly shield the middle part of the right elbow and right hand, in order to seal off the opponent's incoming hand and his advancing technique. Scooping the moon from the sea-bottom, the bright wing changes; flick up and strike the soft ribs, showing no mercy. The scoop-the-moon-from-the-sea-bottom posture takes the strength of the waist as the main pivot of the whole body's activity: that is, when the opponent kicks with his foot, one must quickly lower the body and scoop up his incoming leg, and at the same moment change into the white-crane-spreads-its-wings posture.

Flicking up and striking the soft ribs is a use of white-crane-spreads-its-wings. If the opponent strikes at one's head with his right hand, I quickly use my left hand and arm to flick upward to meet it. At the same moment as the flicking and propping, I advance my right hand and strike horizontally straight at the opponent's soft ribs; the movement must be swift, so that the opponent has no time to answer with his own hand and is struck and sent out.

Brush-the-knee twist-step seeks within the oblique; hand-plays-the-lute pierces and transforms with fineness. If the opponent kicks with his foot, and it comes no higher than my knee, I quickly bend the body and brush it downward with my hand. When brushing in this posture one must "brush big". What is called "brushing big" is a defensive method that guards the whole front of the body and at the same time has regard for below the knee. For while brushing, the opponent may strike at the same moment at my chest and belly or straight at my face, so one cannot use the single method of guarding against the kick alone: while brushing and meeting the opponent's incoming force with the left hand, one must at the same time send the right hand driving straight at the opponent's right shoulder to strike it. The line of advance is diagonal, because "seeking within the oblique" can make the opponent lose his centre of gravity and be struck and sent falling out.

The use of hand-plays-the-lute is this: if the opponent strikes at my true chest with his right hand, I at once ward it aside to the right with my left hand, and at the same time quickly thrust my right hand out from below upward, meeting and holding the opponent's incoming hand, waiting for movement and watching his transformation before advancing a technique.

Clinging close to the body, the horizontal elbow rises; guarding the centre, striking back, one again plays the champion. A proverb says: "At long range the fist, at close range the elbow, on the body the shoulder and hip" — that is, when one is close to the opponent one should use the method of striking with the elbow. For example: while I hold the opponent's hand and arm with both hands and apply the roll-back, the opponent takes advantage of the pull and drag of my force, steps in, and drives his shoulder at my chest in a following-on charge; at this moment I quickly bend my elbow and strike outward horizontally, and the opponent will of himself be sent falling out. Because the opponent charges with his shoulder, his incoming force is straight — or one may say vertical — and I meet it with a bent elbow, meeting it in a horizontal line, so that the opponent's incoming force is discharged outward. This technique occupies one character, "elbow", among the thirteen characters of taijiquan's substance.

Guarding the centre and striking back is a method of using the elbow. It is entirely different from the horizontal-elbow use. For example: when my right hand is in contact with the opponent's right hand, I parry and meet his incoming hand with my left hand from below my right wrist, and at the same time step forward and drive my right elbow-tip at the opponent's chest. The opponent must shield with his left hand; at this moment I use my left hand to follow my right wrist downward and meet it with a brushing, shielding the middle part of the elbow. While brushing and shielding, and taking the elbow-tip as the centre of the circle, I quickly fling my right fist in a circular shape, issuing it back out from in front of my chest to strike at the opponent's face.

Advancing, deflection-block intercepts beneath the ribs; like-seal-like-close guards the centre squarely. If the opponent strikes at the very centre of my chest with his right palm or fist, I quickly use my left hand to deflect and block his elbow, and at the same time advance my right hand, taking the momentum of his attack, to strike beneath his ribs; at the same moment as the right hand advances, the right foot must step up and act in unison with the hand. If the opponent attacks with his left hand, then I deflect and block with my right hand, step up with the left foot, and strike beneath the opponent's ribs with my left hand.

The use of like-seal-like-close is this: if my elbow and wrist are caught by the opponent, I must quickly put out my other hand, meeting straight from the elbow to the wrist, taking the form of covering and parrying. In this way I can withdraw the caught elbow or wrist, and also look for the opportunity to advance a technique and strike. The meaning of "guarding the centre" is to use the form of like-seal-like-close to protect the chest and the bent-elbow portion.

The cross-hand method changes without end; embrace-the-tiger, return-to-the-mountain — pluck and rend complete. The methods of the taijiquan hand can all be produced by change from the crossing of the two hands. The cross-hand method is nothing more than one opening and one closing; there is a method in opening and a method in closing too — that is, the method of one look and one advance. Advance and look must be used at the same moment, with no fast or slow, or else one may be caught unprepared.

"Embrace-the-tiger, return-to-the-mountain" occupies the two characters pluck and rend among the thirteen characters of taijiquan's substance. Pluck means to take, that is, the grasping and rolling-back method: with the strength of the ten fingers, a single hand grasping and rolling back is called pluck; both hands grasping and rolling back is called rend. For example: when my right hand is in contact with the opponent's right hand, I roll his wrist with my right hand and at the same time put out my left hand to strike at his face. The opponent will certainly use his left hand to intercept the left hand I have sent out; at this moment I take the opportunity to roll his left wrist with my left hand, and quickly put out my right hand to grasp and roll his left elbow, pulling and dragging it outward with force, so that the opponent stumbles and falls out.

Elbow-bottom look-at-fist guards the centre hand; retreat three steps and reverse the forearm. "Elbow-bottom look-at-fist" pays attention to guarding the middle joint of the arm. In the martial sense what matters most is guarding the middle joint; as the proverb says, "If the middle joint is not clear, the whole body is empty". For example: when my left arm extends forward, the right hand must be made into a fist and placed beneath the left elbow, so that, should the opponent use a grasp-and-roll, I can use my right hand to grasp and roll and pluck his hand away, thereby attaining the effect of guarding the middle joint.

The posture of reversing the forearm occupies one character, "retreat", among the thirteen main characters of taijiquan.

Sinking the body, retreating, pull and draw back with strength; the oblique-flying technique must not be used in vain. The first line explains the meaning of retreating and turning the two forearms. "Forearm" refers to the arm. If my wrist is grasped and rolled by the opponent, I at once hollow the chest and sink the elbow, and at the same time reverse the wrist, turning the palm upward, so that the opponent's hand is robbed of its power; then, using the strength of the waist and the method of sinking the body, I draw it backward. If the opponent does not advance, well and good; if he wishes to advance, I put out my other hand to intercept and strike him head-on — this is the method of luring the opponent deep and then advancing a technique. In sinking the body and retreating one must use a straight step.

"The oblique-flying technique must not be used in vain": when joining right hands with the opponent, one must roll his wrist, and at the same time step forward behind the opponent and thrust my left hand and the whole arm obliquely upward along the underside of the arm I have rolled, so as to use the oblique propping force of shoulder and arm to rush and prop against the opponent's shoulder and armpit, so that by my oblique propping his body is sent falling out sideways.

Sea-bottom needle requires bowing the body to reach it; fan-through-the-back is a lifting and propping work. "Sea-bottom needle requires bowing the body to reach it": if the opponent attacks downward at my leg, I at once using the strength of the waist, bow the body downward and shield with the hand; with the strength of the waist bow the body down to guard the middle of my leg. Thus one has the stored potential to follow up and advance a technique.

"Fan-through-the-back is a lifting and propping work": in joining and taking up the right hand with the opponent, to use the advancing method that strikes first — that is, at the same moment as joining the right hand, one must swiftly send out the left hand, thrusting it up from below along the underside of the opponent's right arm, and must use the left elbow to issue the force of lifting and propping, propping the opponent's right arm up. When his right ribs are thus exposed, one quickly uses the right palm and the left palm, with the tiger-mouths facing each other, to drive straight in and strike between the opponent's chest and ribs.

Twist-body hammer strikes with a flash-transforming form; crossing the body and advancing, the technique is accomplished. The use of the twist-body hammer is this: if the opponent, using a dodge-and-transform (an extremely swift turning) attacks toward my flank, I counter with the method of the cross-body twist-body hammer, to prevent the opponent from gaining the moment to advance his hand.

"Crossing the body and advancing, the technique is accomplished": this says that if the opponent dodges and transforms my hand aside and strikes toward my flank, I step forward, cross my body, and strike the opponent's chest with the twist-body hammer.

"Within the wrist there is in fact a closing and seizing method": for example, when my right wrist is seized and rolled by the opponent's right hand, I must quickly use my left hand to press down with force the opponent's right hand that is rolling mine; at the same time I sink the body and pull the opponent's hand downward, making his body bend forward and downward. At this moment I turn the seized right wrist up and out, and immediately drive it beneath the opponent's chin to prop and strike. In turning out the right wrist, the left hand must press the opponent's hand firmly.

"Cloud-hands thrice advance to attack upon the arm": when joining right hands with the opponent, one uses the left hand to thrust up from below the opponent's right arm, and with the left shoulder props straight against the opponent's right armpit. When the opponent's right arm is slightly propped up, one quickly uses force in a reverse shake, and my left hand sweeps outward horizontally. Note that in this hand method the point of force lies in the reverse shake; it occupies one character, "advance", among the thirteen characters of taijiquan.

"High-pat-the-horse intercepts the hand and pierces": if the opponent strikes at the very centre of my chest with one or both hands, I use my left hand and arm to sweep horizontally downward from above, intercepting the incoming hand, and at the same time pierce straight at the opponent's face with my right palm (palm upward).

"Left and right separate-the-foot, the hands must seal": that is, when I am about to kick the opponent's ribs with my foot, I must first intercept the opponent's hand with my own hand before making the technique; in this way I can guard against the danger of my leg being scooped up by the opponent.

Turn the body and kick with the leg, occupying the belly; advancing, plant-the-hammer, charging head-on. "Turn the body and kick with the leg, occupying the belly": when crossing hands with the opponent, if there is no opportunity to use a hand technique, one quickly turns the body and puts out the leg, driving straight at the opponent's belly to kick and stamp upon it. This is also a technique of luring the enemy deep and turning defeat into victory.

"Advancing, plant-the-hammer, charging head-on": the use described in this line is much the same as the method of high-pat-the-horse, except that the method of striking out the hand differs. For example: if the opponent strikes at my true chest with his hand, I quickly use hand and arm to sweep horizontally downward from above against the opponent's incoming hand; at the same time the other hand swings up from below backward and turns into a fist, driving straight from behind the calf at the opponent's face. When swinging the fist out from behind the calf, one must lower the head and bend the waist to follow and increase the strength of the arm; and one must take note that when using this hammer it must be used while facing the opponent, so that it does not fail to strike whether to left or right.

Reverse the body as the white snake spits its tongue and changes; pluck the enemy's hand and take his two pupils. In the practice forms there is a hand method in which the body reverses and the white snake spits its tongue: when joining right hands with the opponent, one plucks his right hand and rolls it downward, and quickly sends out the left hand to pierce straight at the opponent's eyes. This hand method is "pluck the enemy's hand and take his two pupils".

Right heel-kick upward at the soft ribs; left and right drape-the-body, the tiger-taming essence. "Right heel-kick upward at the soft ribs": after I have sealed the opponent's hand with my own, I quickly put out my leg and kick straight at the opponent's ribs.

"Left and right drape-the-body, the tiger-taming essence": for example, when joining right hands with the opponent, one slightly plucks and rolls the opponent's wrist, then quickly withdraws the hand and turns it into a fist, and steps with the leg to the opponent's right side, striking the opponent's breast with the fist. While stepping forward to the opponent's right side, one circles up the left arm and strikes with the fist at the lower-right side of the opponent's rear arm; the left fist and right fist must cross and strike against each other. Left and right use the same method, and so it is called "left and right drape-the-body, the tiger-taming essence".

Above, strike the true chest; below, use at the ribs; twin-wind-pierces-the-ears, the technique is wondrous. "Above, strike the true chest; below, use at the ribs": this explains the use of drape-the-body and tiger-taming — that is, upward one may strike the opponent's breast, and downward one may strike the soft ribs.

"Twin-wind-pierces-the-ears, the technique is wondrous": when I use the press form upon the opponent, if he uses both hands to press down from between my two hands, I follow the force of his downward press, turn both hands upward and make them into fists, and drive straight at the opponent's ears, striking fiercely with the two fists held opposite each other.

"Turning the body, kick with the heel to meet the knee-bone": when crossing hands with the opponent, if his attacking momentum comes too close and there is no time to answer with a technique, I turn the body to evade at once, and quickly use the foot in a flat-kick manner to kick and stamp at the soft cartilage below the opponent's knee.

Wild-horse-parts-its-mane attacks beneath the armpit; jade-maid-works-the-shuttles seals the four corners. "Wild-horse-parts-its-mane attacks beneath the armpit": when joining hands with the opponent, if my hand is seized and rolled by him, I quickly draw my body slightly back, and at the same time use my other hand (palm upward), following the momentum of the forward step, to prop and strike straight along the underside of the opponent's arm into his armpit.

"Jade-maid-works-the-shuttles seals the four corners": this hand method is used when pushing hands with the opponent. If the opponent's ward-off form is very tight, and I cannot gain the moment of empty and full to advance my hand, I must use my right hand to press the opponent's left elbow, follow his neutralizing force, thrust through beneath the elbow, then with the tiger-mouth upward prop the opponent's upper arm up, and quickly step forward and strike between the opponent's ribs with my left hand. In the practice form one walks in the four corner directions, and so it is said that the jade maid works the shuttles and seals the four corners.

This line explains the use of jade-maid-works-the-shuttles: the emphasis must be on taking the opponent's single arm, and using the passing method to pluck, transform and prop his single arm up, then striking in at the ribs. The use on left and right is the same.

For example: if the opponent strikes at me with a technique from above downward, I quickly follow his incoming momentum downward and cling to it, waiting until the opponent's downward strike falls empty; then I use the clinging hand (at the same time using the strength of the three joints — waist, hip and knee — to drive forward) to flick and strike the opponent's genitals or belly.

It has two aspects in use: for example, when locked together with the opponent, my hand lifts the opponent's hand and arm from above, or scoops and presses his waist, I may quickly raise one leg and drive the knee straight at the opponent's crotch; or again, when locked with the opponent and unable to separate, I may quickly lift one leg, aim the heel at the opponent's instep and stamp down with fierce force, at the same time lifting the other leg up, in order to increase the force of the downward stamp, so that the whole body's weight is dropped onto one heel (that is, stamp down with the right foot, quickly lift the left leg, and bring the whole body's force down in the stamping blow — also called "the thousand-catty drop").

Lift the knee and strike high at the vital spot; below, injure the two feet, sparing nothing. This line explains that the method of golden-rooster-stands-on-one-leg is very fierce in use. If one drives the raised knee at the opponent's crotch, the opponent is in danger of his life; if one uses the technique of the heel's downward stamp, the opponent is in danger of injury to both feet.

The cross-leg method breaks the soft bone; point-to-the-crotch hammer, leaning down, is the blade. In the use of the cross-leg method, when using it one takes the sole of my foot and cuts straight up from below at the soft cartilage below the opponent's knee; when cutting, the height must not exceed the knee portion of the opponent.

If I use the point-to-the-crotch hammer hand method and the opponent seizes and rolls my arm and drags it downward, I follow the opponent's rolling momentum, quickly step forward and drive my shoulder straight at the opponent's chest with force, leaning to strike. This technique occupies one character, "shoulder", among the thirteen characters of taijiquan's substance.

Step-forward-seven-stars, the frame-hand form; step-back-ride-the-tiger dodges to the centre. The use of step-forward-seven-stars is the same as that of the cross-hand, being for the sake of taking up a defensive form; the hand methods used are all produced by change from within the cross. The seven stars refer to the seven striking points: shoulder, elbow, knee, hip, head, hand and foot. In use, apart from the head, which must not be used at random, the hand may strike, the elbow may thrust, the knee may ram, the foot may kick, and shoulder and hip may lean and strike. Therefore when crossing hands with the opponent one must be carefully on guard against these seven points, lest the opponent seize the gap and attack. The frame-hand form explains that when the opponent and I join hands and cross frames, one must use the above seven points as the most essential parts of attack and defence.

"Step-back-ride-the-tiger dodges to the centre": when drawing near to the opponent, if his advancing momentum is too fierce and there is no time to answer with the hand, I quickly step back from the centre point and dodge out of the way, and then take advantage of the opponent's incoming momentum to strike at the point where he may be attacked.

"Turn the body swing-the-lotus, guard the leg and advance": this explains the points to note when using the leg. For example, when joining hands with the opponent, one must first use both hands to wrench the opponent's left arm open to the right, and then quickly kick with the right foot at the opponent's left ribs. If one does not wrench it open, there is the danger that my leg will be scooped up by the opponent.

"Bend-the-bow-shoot-the-tiger, flick and strike the chest": for example, when the opponent attacks with his right hand, I use my left arm to flick up the opponent's incoming arm, and at the same time step forward and drive out the right fist to strike straight at the opponent's chest. If the opponent's hand form strikes from above downward, one may also use this method to counter-strike.

Like-seal-like-close, watch and settle; the taiji closing-hand form is complete. In applying the like-seal-like-close hand method, one must watch over one's own three-fronts: the front of the hand, the front of the foot, the front of the eye; and expect the seven stars — that is, pay attention to the opponent's seven striking points: shoulder, elbow, knee, hip, head, hand and foot. Settling is central settling — that is, steadying the spirit and mind, and remaining calm and composed. When taijiquan reaches the taiji closing-hand form it is already complete. In practising the boxing one must not only know the practice method but also understand the application, and train with body and use combined; only then can one attain the great accomplishment of taijiquan.

The great function of the whole body takes intent as its master; the body relaxed, the qi solid, the spirit must be congealed. "The great function of the whole body takes intent as its master": this line explains that the whole set of taijiquan practice takes intent as its main substance. Intent means using consciousness to govern the movements — in physiological terms, the leading role of the central nervous system. One may also say that practising taijiquan is to strengthen the reflex actions that the central nervous system produces within the body and its sensitive employment against outside stimuli. The boxing formula says: "In practice it is present within the feeling; in use the form contains it." Both "present within the feeling" and "the form contains it" are the work of intent: "present within the feeling" is the manifestation of the leading activity of the nerve centres; "the form contains it" is the employment that completes a unified activity — that is, the process of practice that forms a conditioned reflex.

"The body relaxed, the qi solid, the spirit must be congealed": this points out the three main essentials and stages of practising taijiquan. Body relaxed means that in practice the whole body, high and low, uses no clumsy force (deliberate stupid strength) but moves easily and naturally; on the other hand one must open the joints and make the postures expansive. Such loosening activity can naturally produce a strength in the sinews and bones; when this strength is issued it is generally called startling force, or elastic force. And because the joints are loosened, one can also extend the length of the four limbs. Qi solid means that in practice the breathing is natural, one must not hold the breath, and the movements and the breathing naturally coordinate, attaining the respiratory function of "changing the breath according to the posture". When strength and qi are joined and the intent guards the elixir field, so that they are not scattered without limit — only thus can the qi be solid.

"The spirit must be congealed" means the intent must be concentrated and the spirit must have something to look at: the movement of each posture must have a point on which to gaze. To attain this stage is inseparable from the employment of the hand methods of "the great function of the whole body". If one does not know the use of each posture and the meaning within the movement, then it is not easy to attain the joining of intent and qi with a methodical exit and entry. In practising taijiquan, if one receives the transmission of these three great essentials and perseveres in training, one can attain definite results.

This formula sets out the methods of ward-off, roll-back, press, push, pluck, rend, elbow, shoulder, advance, retreat, look, expect and settle.

With the ward-off hand both arms must roundly prop; still or moving, empty or full, attack at will. In the ward-off posture both arms must prop roundly, at a height level with the shoulders, forming a circle. There are single ward-off and double ward-off: in single ward-off the left or right arm is raised level with the shoulder, forming a semicircle, with the fingers level with the shoulder-tip; in double ward-off both arms move at once as described above. The main meaning of propping is this: beneath the armpits of the two shoulders it is as though two springs were placed, and the two arms as though upon those springs, propped up by the force of the springs — press them and they fall, lift them and they rise, rising as the opponent rises and lowering as he lowers. On attack they can issue a spring-like elastic force: that is, with a vigilant driving power they snap and spring outward. This is called elastic force. Such elastic force must be contained and not revealed when still, and issued when moving; one must be able to issue the moment the opportunity comes, and to guard when the opportunity does not come. Once the employment of this force-road has been made one's own, in grappling with an opponent there is at all times a reflex action of force. As the proverb says: when the force is not being issued it is like cotton wrapped around iron — soft within and hard, attaining the state of being soft yet able to be hard, and of hard and soft aiding each other. This spring-like propping vitality can be applied within stillness, movement, emptiness and fullness.

Joining hands, roll-back opens, the press-palm is used; the enemy wishes to answer the technique, but his momentum is hard to bring off. This line explains the use of the press hand method. When joining right hands with the opponent, one presses his right wrist with the right hand, and at the same time presses his elbow with the left hand, rolling his incoming momentum toward the side. This deprives the opponent's ward-off form of its effect. At this moment one quickly turns out the left hand and strikes with the back of the left hand beneath the opponent's right shoulder, and presses the left wrist with the right hand, adding an assisting force to the issuing strike — that is, using the strength of both hands issued together. In this way one makes it hard for the opponent to answer with his hand.

With the push-hand technique he seems to topple; seize the second hand and do not let it go. "With the push-hand technique he seems to topple": pushing out with both hands together is called push in taijiquan. The use is to push both hands toward the opponent's chest above and belly below, or to use it after his ward-off form has already been closed off. In use, the two hands push in, one quickly steps forward and drives into the opponent's crotch, and at the same time uses the form of the body's plunging forward to multiply the pushing force — that is, applying the strength of the whole body upon the opponent's body.

"Seize the second hand and do not let it go": this explains that when joining hands with the opponent, if one has grasped and rolled his arm, one must not loosen it, must not let the opponent's wrist come free, so as to be able to use one's own hand method to advance and take.

If his force is fierce, use the rending hand; elbow and shoulder, at any moment, act at will. "If his force is fierce, use the rending hand": in the rending hand method, if the opponent's advancing momentum is too fierce, I quickly use one hand to seek his wrist and one hand to seek his elbow; and if I get hold, I quickly drag with force to the side and rear.

"Elbow and shoulder, at any moment, act at will": that is, if I use the rending method and roll and drag the opponent's body over, but the opponent takes advantage of the force of my rolling and drag to charge and lean toward me with his shoulder, I quickly take the hand that has seized the opponent's elbow, follow the momentum of his leaning strike, and strike outward horizontally with the bending-elbow technique, and turn the body sideways to increase the force of the horizontal elbow, so that the opponent is struck and falls out by the elbow and shoulder.

This line explains that once pluck, rend, elbow and shoulder can all be used with skill, then when crossing hands with the opponent one can do as one's hand wills. Though the opponent's art be high, one has nothing to fear.

Meeting the enemy, step up and press close to strike; guard the three-fronts and look to the seven-stars. The boxing formula says: "Against a nearby man, first advance the body; only when body and hand arrive together is it true." That is, since one does not fear the enemy, one does not avoid him. To fear and hesitate is the greatest defect in martial technique. Therefore, when crossing hands with the opponent, one must be able to step up and press close; only when one can press close to the opponent can one issue a technique and advance the hand, striking first to overpower. But in pressing close one must guard one's own three-fronts and pay attention to the seven parts the opponent uses to attack (guarding the three-fronts and looking to the seven-stars were explained earlier).

If the enemy presses close to strike me, dodge to the centre and strike within the cross. If the opponent, striking first to overpower me, presses close to my body and strikes, I quickly turn the body and dodge out of my own centre portion, so that the opponent's technique falls empty; at the same time I quickly strike at the opponent's flank — that is, I take the opponent's transverse line and, seizing his transverse line, use it as my own central line for attack.

In the method of the thirteen characters of taiji, ponder the essential meaning with care, and the wonder will be reborn. This line explains that the body-and-use method of taijiquan has thirteen characters as its substance. Each of these thirteen characters has its method, and each method has its use. One must cut and polish, chisel and grind, and put in the effort before one can obtain its wondrous use.

Meeting a hand, on encountering ward-off do not enter the coil; sticking and adhering without separation, to gain the technique is hard. In pushing hands, if one meets an opponent whose ward-off form is very tight and whose spring-like sensitivity beneath the armpits is very keen, and whose skill in following movement is also very strict, then the only course is to adopt the method of entering the hand, so that the opponent cannot answer with his hand. If one merely sticks and adheres without separation (listening to force), it is not easy to gain the advantage.

To close the ward-off one must use the pluck-and-rend method; if the second hand is secured, he has no time for aid. In joining with the opponent, if one meets a ward-off form that is not easy to penetrate, one must use the pluck-and-rend hand method; and if one has already grasped and rolled him, one must quickly drag him backward or seize the moment to advance another hand method. For delay brings change and doubt brings slowness, and either way it is easy for the opponent to seize the moment and attack in.

Push firmly on the four cardinal and four corner directions, then change; touch the hand and at once occupy the first of the first. The four cardinals are the four square directions; the four corners are the four diagonal angles. In crossing hands with the opponent, on the one hand I must actively hold and control the four cardinals, and on the other I must seek the opponent's four corners — the purpose being to shift the opponent's cardinals and first break the force at his centre. If the opponent guards his four cardinals, I then contrive to change my own cardinals and seek out the opponent's four corners to attack. In sum: using my front to exchange for the opponent's side — and so it is called "push firmly on the four cardinal and four corner directions, then change". "Touch the hand and at once occupy the first of the first" explains the manner of striking first to overpower, not the manner of awaiting movement in stillness. If the opponent is too far from me, then of course one awaits movement in stillness; but if he has already come near, or one happens to have joined hands, then one should strike first to overpower.

Roll-back and press, the two methods, seize the moment to use; elbow and shoulder attack at the front of the foot. In taijiquan, roll-back and press are counted as one method, for where there is roll-back there must be press: for example, having rolled the opponent's ward-off form open, one may take the momentum to use press and attack. "Elbow and shoulder" explains that when using elbow, shoulder, or hip to lean and ram against the opponent, one must attack in front of the opponent's heel: if on the opponent's left side, advance the right foot; if on his right side, advance the left foot.

Meeting the moment and gaining the advantage, advance and retreat; the three-fronts and the seven-stars lie between look and expect. This line explains that in attack and defence with the opponent, one must apply advance and retreat according to the moment: advance when one should advance, retreat when one should retreat — one must have a method in advance and retreat. How then does one advance? Advancing means advancing the body, advancing the step, advancing the hand; it is the manner of striking first to overpower and not being controlled by the opponent. Retreating is dodging, unfolding, rising and shifting; one must observe the opponent's incoming momentum and transform flexibly — it is the manner of taking the defensive. In walking one must achieve turning and transformation, moving through without obstruction. (The three-fronts and the seven-stars between look and expect were explained earlier.)

The whole body's real strength is settled within the intent; listening, probing, yielding and transforming are the gate of spirit and qi. "The whole body's real strength is settled within the intent": this explains the method of issuing the vertical strength of the whole body. The greater part of the whole body may be divided into three sections: the heel is the root section, the waist and hip are the middle section, and the head, neck and shoulder are the tip section. These three sections are the key points for issuing the whole body's real strength. As the proverb says: "When the root section moves, the tip section and the three sections arrive together, increasing the force." When the three sections move together, the intent must surely be placed on the waist — that is, the mastery of the three sections' force lies in the waist. If the waist does not move, then though the root and tip sections move, they cannot issue a whole force but only a local force. Therefore in martial technique the waist must be the main force, and only then can one issue the whole body's real strength.

"Listening, probing, yielding and transforming are the gate of spirit and qi": the "listening force" in taijiquan is a perception produced by the rubbing and grinding of the wrist, elbow and arm; it is a method convenient for both attack and defence. On joining hands with the opponent, one senses where the opponent's force is hard or soft and the direction of the movement of each part, and can then neutralize the opponent's incoming momentum and, seizing the moment, follow the momentum to issue a technique and strike at him. This is the manner of attack by means of adhering, sticking, following and connecting. The listener is not one who wholly awaits movement in stillness: he is within the movement, responding with composure and seizing the moment to advance. Probing is, within composed response, to discern the opponent's empty and full: if he can be attacked, one strikes with the force of a thunderclap that leaves no time to stop the ears; if one should guard, one stores the intent and awaits the moment. Yielding is not to resist the opponent's incoming momentum head-on; within following and yielding one keeps the intent of secretly pulling him: one may say one follows his momentum and takes his method. Following the rising and falling, advancing and retreating of the opponent's incoming momentum, one transforms with the momentum — this is the meaning of neither abandoning nor resisting, making it so that the opponent cannot do as he pleases, and attaining that which I take is without obstruction. Transforming is the method of dividing and turning the opponent's incoming momentum and taking the initiative: to neutralize his incoming momentum and advance my own technique; meeting the empty to become full, meeting the full to become empty; using softness to neutralize hardness, with hardness following closely behind; using hardness to drive softness, with softness not losing its firmness (firmness is steadiness and firmness, the very substance of softness, not weakness without power).

The meaning of "the gate of spirit and qi" is that, in the course of employing listening, probing, yielding and transforming, one must concentrate the whole spirit and not be indolent, so that one does not miss the moment or fall into confusion, and so that one may respond with ease. Without settling the qi, this cannot be: if one is tense and panicky, the qi must float upward, and once the qi floats up the movements err, and one is easily taken by the opponent. What the taijiquan treatises call "the intent is lord, flesh and blood are ministers", "use the intent to move the qi, use the qi to thread the spirit", and "the spirit threads the crown" — this is the same principle.

Seeing the real but not attacking, one gains no striking hand; what day will one's skill be complete in body and use? In the employment of empty and full, if one has already gained the entry — that is, when the hand has already gained the real — one must at once release the hand and issue the technique, and must not miss the opportunity. If one has the real but does not immediately issue the technique, then it becomes a falling-empty, with body but no use.

Practising without applying according to the body, one may cultivate to the end and still the art will not be fine. One who practises taijiquan must understand body and use. When practising the boxing one obtains the effect of health and a force-road that arises of itself — this may count as having obtained the fundamental skill of this boxing. Only when one can further give play to the meaning of the boxing and apply it in martial technique may one count as having gathered its great accomplishment. As the proverb says, "Skill is easy to train, but force is hard to obtain", and "Better to lengthen the sinews by one inch than to thicken the flesh by one inch" — there is reason in this.

Few are those who cultivate the yin and yang of taiji; swallowing and spitting, opening and closing, ask of the hard and soft. Yin and yang contain two opposed natures and phenomena: for example, forward and reverse, soft and hard, hard and soft, extending and bending, up and down, left and right, front and rear, and so on. Many practise taijiquan, but few study this principle even now.

Swallowing-and-spitting and hard-and-soft occupy two methods among the thirteen methods of taijiquan. These two methods are employed within the opening and closing movements. For example, in pushing the four hands level, called the closing hand, one takes the spitting method as primary and the swallowing method as secondary, with softness in the lead and hard force following closely behind, using the pushing method as much as possible. This line explains that after crossing hands with the opponent, by the division of opening and closing one employs the methods of swallowing and spitting and gives play to the forces of hard and soft. Hard and soft are yin and yang: the hard belongs to yang, the soft to yin. That is, the division of opening and closing, swallowing and spitting, accords with the principle of yin and yang, empty and full.

Cardinal and corner, gathering and releasing, let the lord walk where he will; stillness and movement, transformation — why need one worry? Cardinal-and-corner and gathering-and-releasing occupy two methods among the thirteen methods of taijiquan. The cardinals are the four square directions, the corners are the four angles. To attack from my cardinal toward the opponent's corner is the necessary path in hand technique. If cardinal meets cardinal there will be collision (also called committing double-weighting), and the two will resist each other with force; if corner meets corner one commits the fault of "light meeting light and all falling empty". One must strike the light with the heavy and avoid the heavy with the light, and only then does one not lose the road to victory.

Gathering and releasing: first to lure the opponent deep — that is, the common saying "to seize, first let go". Gathering is first to bring in toward the bosom; releasing is to take the momentum and let the hand strike out. This line explains that in crossing hands with the opponent one must make use of the opportunities of the cardinal and the corner directions to advance and retreat, and then strike in according to the methods of gathering and releasing, dealing with him calmly amid the changes of stillness and movement, and responding according to the moment.

The two methods of generation and overcoming are used as occasion serves; dodging and advancing lie wholly in seeking within movement. In using hand methods there is the generating hand — that is, the hand method of striking first to overpower; and there is the overcoming hand — that is, the hand method of guarding against the opponent's advance and strike. Generating hand and overcoming hand must be applied in close coordination.

If the opponent's incoming momentum is fierce and there is no time to answer with a technique, one must first dodge his point of advance; but though it is a dodge, one must not lose the room to answer with the hand. This explains that dodging-and-advancing and looking-and-striking are a single action and must not be cut apart and used separately. These all require constant attention within movement.

How does one tell light from heavy, empty from full? When lightness appears within heaviness, do not linger a moment. In the wrestling methods and striking methods of the martial arts, the employment of force and strength comes to no more than three forces: light, heavy, and empty. The key problem here lies in the change of light and heavy, empty and full.

The change of light and heavy in the martial arts is inseparable from empty and full. If, crossing hands with the opponent, one perceives that lightness has appeared within heaviness, one should at once issue the hand; so long as one does not resist the force, one must follow the direction of the force and advance, and thus the strike is never but hits. In actual use, the meaning of single-weighting and double-weighting does not lie in whether the centre of gravity is placed on the front leg or the rear leg, but in the starting point of the employment of force. What is called "following the heavy to use the light, following the light to employ the heavy" — only thus can the efficacy of light and heavy be given play. The boxing formula says: "Double-weighting will not get through; single-weighting succeeds instead." That is: double-weighting is force against force, locked in a stand-off; I wish to go, you wish to come, and the result is that the greater force overcomes the smaller. Single-weighting is this: when I strike out with force, I make the opponent lose his power of resistance and attain my aim of issuing a technique and using the hand. To achieve this kind of skill one must have the wholly skilful skill of "following". If single-weighting is used well, one can make the opponent fall into emptiness. And so it is said: "Better to use the heavy than the light, but better to use the light than the empty." To be able to cause the opponent to fall empty everywhere — in the martial art this is the highest master of all.

The light and heavy spoken of here are the distinguishing of single and double. The force spoken of in taijiquan is central force and the force of the centre of gravity. In martial use, it is using my centre-of-gravity force to strike at the opponent's centre. To put it briefly: strike the centre with the heavy, use the hand with settle.

1. Practice method:

2. Technical meaning:

Ward-off is to make the left or right arm into a semicircle in front of the chest. If the left hand makes the form, then the fingers are aimed at the right shoulder-tip and the other hand guards the middle joint of the ward-off arm, serving to shield the true chest and to avoid the opponent's advancing hand method.

Roll-back is to roll the opponent's elbow with one hand and his wrist with the other, rolling them diagonally downward following the direction of his incoming force: it is the use of breaking the opponent's ward-off form and luring him in and letting him fall empty.

Press is to press my left hand upon the inner pulse of the right wrist while striking out straight with the back of the right hand at the opponent's below-shoulder above-elbow; the force of the two hands is joined and issued together.

Push is the method of aiming both hands at the opponent's ward-off form, and at the moment when my hands have gained a firm purchase, pushing out with force and advancing the step.

The formula (the Formula of the Great Function of the Whole Body — the same below) says: "From ward-off, roll-back, press and push the sparrow-tail is born" (see the earlier note).

Oblique single whip is: when the opponent strikes with one hand, I hook and roll his wrist with my hand, and at the same time step forward and strike at his true chest with the other hand. The formula says: "Walking obliquely, single-whip occupies the chest" (see the earlier note).
