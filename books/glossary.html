Tai Chi Glossary

Eight Gates · Five Steps · Principles · Push Hands · Body Method · Stances

This page collects the core terms and concepts that appear throughout our book translations. Each entry gives the Chinese character, pinyin romanisation, its Vietnamese Han-Viet reading, and a plain-language definition. The Han-Viet readings are preserved as they appear in the Vietnamese texts, so you can match them directly against the books.

66 terms · 9 categories

Eight Gates · 八法 Bā Fǎ

掤

péng / bīng
Bằng

Ward-off. An expanding, upward-and-outward force, like water supporting a floating log. Not rigid resistance — it keeps contact while redirecting.

捋

lǚ
Lãm / Loa

Rollback. Yielding and drawing the opponent's force past you, emptying his attack by guiding it aside rather than meeting it.

挤

jǐ
Tế

Press. Squeezing forward from close range, typically with the forearm grating against the opponent's arm.

按

àn
Án

Push. Pressing down on the opponent's arms and then driving forward to break his root.

采

cǎi
Thái

Pluck. A sharp downward-and-sideways grasp, like picking fruit, used to break balance.

挒

liè
Liệt

Split. A spiralling, shearing wrench that splits the opponent's structure apart.

肘

zhǒu
Chẩu

Elbow. Striking with the elbow point at close range, where the hand can no longer be used.

靠

kào
Khao

Bump. Striking with shoulder, back or hip, powered by the waist and legs, at the closest range.

Five Steps · 五步 Wǔ Bù

进

jìn
Tiến

Advance. Moving the weight forward, stepping in to occupy the opponent's space.

退

tuì
Thối

Retreat. Withdrawing the weight rearward to yield and draw the opponent in.

顾

gù
Cố

Look left. Awareness and movement toward the left flank.

盼

pàn
Phán

Look right. Awareness and movement toward the right flank.

定

dìng
Định

Central equilibrium. Standing still with centred balance — the root from which all movement springs.

Core Principles · 核心原理

太极

tàijí
Thái Cực

The Supreme Ultimate — the source polarity from which yin and yang, movement and stillness, arise.

阴阳

yīnyáng
Âm Dương

Complementary opposites: hard/soft, full/empty, open/closed. Tai Chi skill is the ability to hold and switch between them.

气

qì
Khí

Vital energy or breath. In practice, the felt sense of internal activation that follows correct alignment and relaxation.

丹田

dāntián
Đan Điền

The elixir field, about three finger-widths below the navel — the body's physical and energetic centre of gravity.

松

sōng
Tùng / Tống

Relaxation with structure — letting go of unnecessary tension while staying connected and upright. The prerequisite for everything else.

沉

chén
Trầm

Sinking. Settling the weight and the qi downward so the stance roots and the upper body stays light.

意

yì
Ý

Intent. Where the mind leads, the qi follows; in Tai Chi the movement is directed by intent, not by muscular effort.

劲

jìn
Kình

Trained, coordinated force — whole-body power released as a unit. Distinct from brute muscular strength.

力

lì
Lực

Raw, local muscular strength. The classics treat relying on li as the beginner's error.

虚实

xūshí
Hư Thực

Empty and full. Weight distribution between the legs; the art of being empty where the opponent presses and full where he is open.

中定

zhōngdìng
Trung Định

Central equilibrium — the still centre that all thirteen postures depend on. Lose it and every technique fails.

刚柔

gāngróu
Cang Nhu

Hard and soft. Softness conceals hardness; hardness is released from softness. Neither exists alone.

开合

kāihé
Khai Hợp

Opening and closing. The breath-like expansion and contraction running through every movement of the form.

Push Hands · 推手 Tuī Shǒu

推手

tuīshǒu
Thôi Thủ

Push hands. The two-person sensitivity drill that turns solo form practice into a live test of timing, balance and listening.

听劲

tīngjìn
Thính Kình

Listening energy — reading the opponent's force, direction and intent through contact, before it becomes a visible move.

懂劲

dǒngjìn
Đổng Kình

Understanding energy. The stage after listening, where you can interpret what you feel and answer it correctly.

化劲

huàjìn
Hóa Kình

Neutralising force. Dissolving an incoming attack by redirecting it, so it arrives nowhere.

发劲

fājìn
Phát Kình

Issuing force. Releasing stored whole-body power in an instant, at the moment the opponent has lost his root.

粘黏连随

nián nián lián suí
Niêm Liên Tùy

Stick, adhere, connect, follow. The four qualities of contact: never separate, never resist, always stay with the opponent.

不丢不顶

bù diū bù dǐng
Bất Đâu Bất Đảnh

Neither letting go nor resisting. The central rule of push hands — stay in contact without opposing force with force.

引进落空

yǐn jìn luò kōng
Dẫn Tiến Lạc Không

Lead him in, let him fall into emptiness. Draw the attack further than intended so the opponent's own momentum defeats him.

借力打力

jiè lì dǎ lì
Mượn Lực Đả Lực

Borrow the opponent's force to strike him. The core economy of Tai Chi: the answer comes from what he gives you.

四两拨千斤

sì liǎng bō qiān jīn
Tứ Lượng Bát Thiên Cân

Four ounces deflect a thousand pounds. The classic image of yielding overcoming force through correct timing and angle.

Body Method · 身法 Shēn Fǎ

虚领顶劲

xū lǐng dǐng jìn
Hư Lĩnh Đỉnh Kình

Suspend the headtop. Lift the crown lightly, as if hung from above, so the neck is free and the spine extends.

含胸拔背

hán xiōng bá bèi
Hàm Hung Bạt Bối

Hollow the chest, raise the back. Round the back slightly so the chest is not pushed out and force can pass through the spine.

沉肩坠肘

chén jiān zhuì zhǒu
Trầm Kiên Trụy Chẩu

Sink the shoulders, drop the elbows. Removes tension from the upper body so the arms are powered by the back, not the shoulder muscles.

松腰松胯

sōng yāo sōng kuà
Tùng Yêu Tùng Khóa

Loosen the waist and hips. The waist is the commander of movement; if it is tight, nothing above or below it works.

气沉丹田

qì chén dāntián
Khí Trầm Đan Điền

Sink the qi to the dantian. Breathe and settle into the lower abdomen, lowering the centre of gravity.

上下相随

shàng xià xiāng suí
Thượng Hạ Tương Tùy

Upper and lower follow one another. Hands, waist and feet move as one; nothing leads or lags.

内外相合

nèi wài xiāng hé
Nội Ngoại Tương Hợp

Inner and outer unite. Intent, breath and internal movement match the external shape of the posture.

连绵不断

lián mián bù duàn
Liên Miên Bất Đoạn

Continuous, like a river. The form flows without break or pause; each posture grows out of the last.

用意不用力

yòng yì bù yòng lì
Dụng Ý Bất Dụng Lực

Use intent, not strength. The classic instruction to direct movement with the mind and abandon muscular forcing.

Stances · 步法 Bù Fǎ

弓步

gōngbù
Cung Bộ

Bow stance. Front leg bent, rear leg straightened, weight mostly forward — the standard attacking stance.

马步

mǎbù
Mã Bộ

Horse-riding stance. Feet parallel and wide, knees bent, weight centred — builds leg power and roots the structure.

虚步

xūbù
Hư Bộ

Empty stance. Weight on the rear leg, front foot lightly touching — ready to kick, step or yield.

半马步

bànmǎbù
Bán Mã Bộ

Half-horse stance. An intermediate stance between bow and horse, weight shared but favouring one leg.

独立步

dúlìbù
Độc Lập Bộ

Single-leg stance. Standing on one leg with the other lifted — the test of balance and rootedness.

横裆步

héngdāngbù
Hoành Đang Bộ

Horizontal-crotch stance. A wide side-on stance with the knees opened, used in the paired routines.

Hand Forms · 手法 Shǒu Fǎ

掌

zhǎng
Chưởng

Open palm. The most-used hand shape in Tai Chi — relaxed, slightly cupped, fingers extended but soft.

拳

quán
Quyền

Fist. Loosely closed, not clenched hard; the fist is formed at the moment of striking.

勾

gōu
Câu

Hook hand. Fingers gathered and wrist bent downward, used in a few transitional postures.

虎口

hǔkǒu
Hổ Khẩu

Tiger mouth — the web between thumb and index finger. Kept rounded and open, never collapsed flat.

立掌

lìzhǎng
Lập Chưởng

Vertical palm. Palm held upright with the thumb on top, used for pressing and parrying.

Training · 练法 Liàn Fǎ

站桩

zhànzhuāng
Trạm Trang

Standing post. Holding a static posture to build structure, root, breath and patience before moving.

套路

tàolù
Thao Lộ / Đường Lộ

The solo form — a fixed sequence of postures practised as one continuous movement.

对练

duìliàn
Đối Luyện

Paired practice. A choreographed two-person set that gives each technique its real combat meaning.

散手

sànshǒu
Tán Thủ

Free-hand sparring. Applying the form's principles without a fixed sequence.

缠丝劲

chánsījìn
Triền Ty Kình

Silk-reeling energy. The spiralling, screwing motion running through all Chen-style movement.

Family Styles · 五大流派

陈式

Chén shì
Trần thức

Chen style. The oldest documented style, from Chenjiagou. Low stances, spiralling silk-reeling, bursts of power alternating with slow movement.

杨式

Yáng shì
Dương thức

Yang style. From Yang Luchan. Large, upright, even-paced and gentle — the most widely practised style in the world.

吴式

Wú shì
Ngô thức

Wu style. Small, compact frame with a distinctive forward-leaning posture and soft, continuous pushing.

武式

Wǔ shì
Võ thức

Wu (Hao) style. Compact, internal, small-circle; emphasises internal cultivation and precise body alignment.

孙式

Sūn shì
Tôn thức

Sun style. Agile, with lively following steps and an open, high frame; blends Tai Chi with Xingyi and Bagua.
