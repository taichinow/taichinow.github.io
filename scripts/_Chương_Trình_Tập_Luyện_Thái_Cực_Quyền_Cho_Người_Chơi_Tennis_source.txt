Chương Trình Tập Luyện Thái Cực Quyền Cho
Người Chơi Tennis
As a healthy 52-year-old who plays tennis every day, you already have excellent physical
conditioning. Adding Tai Chi to your routine is a perfect cross-training choice because it
will enhance your balance, core stability, and mental focus, while serving as active
recovery to protect your joints from the high impact of tennis.
For your profile, the **Yang style 24-form** (also known as the Beijing short form) is
highly recommended as it condenses foundational principles into a routine that takes a
few months to learn [1]. Alternatively, you might explore the **Sun style**, which
features a higher stance and agile "follow-stepping," putting less physical pressure on the
knees—a great benefit for someone who plays tennis daily [2, 3].
Here is a structured 1-hour daily practice program tailored for you:
**Phase 1: Warm-Up & Joint Mobility (10 Minutes)**
Before stepping into stances, it is vital to warm up all your joints and ligaments. * **Joint
Rotations:** Start with gentle rotations of your wrists, ankles, knees, hips, shoulders, and
neck [4]. When rotating your knees, use your hands as cups to support and protect them
[4]. * **Loosening (Fang Song):** Focus on deeply relaxing your muscles and joints.
Releasing tension helps prevent the body from moving like a rigid block and prepares your
connective tissues for martial movements [5, 6].

**Phase 2: Foundation & Stance Work (15 Minutes)**
Build your root and internal awareness before moving. * **Standing Meditation (Zhan
Zhuang):** Stand upright with your feet shoulder-width apart, knees slightly bent, and
arms relaxed. Breathe deeply into your lower abdomen (Đan Điền), visualizing roots
growing from your feet into the earth [7, 8]. * **Weight Shifting (Phân rõ Hư Thực):**
Practice shifting your weight slowly from one leg to the other. Ensure you are clearly
distinguishing between your "solid" (weight-bearing) and "empty" (unweighted) legs [9].
Step slowly without committing weight until the foot is placed, avoiding the mistake of
keeping equal weight on both feet [10, 11].
**Phase 3: Form Practice (25 Minutes)**
Dedicate this time to learning and refining specific movements from the Yang 24-form. Do
not rush to learn the whole sequence; focus on 1-2 movements at a time [12, 13]. *
**Starting Posture (Qi Shi):** Stand upright, relax your shoulders, tuck your chin slightly,
and let your arms hang naturally [7]. Raise your arms slowly and press them down to
waist level [14]. * **Part the Wild Horse's Mane:** Shift weight completely to your
support leg, step out heel-to-toe with your empty leg, and expand your arms softly [11,
15]. * **Brush Knee and Twist Step:** Step forward while keeping your torso upright. Use
your waist (Đan Điền) to lead the rotation rather than your shoulders [16]. * **Cloud
Hands:** Shift your weight side-to-side and rotate your waist, moving your hands in a
horizontal circle. Coordinate this with your breath: inhale as hands rise, exhale as they
fall [17]. * **Repulse the Monkey:** Step backward smoothly while making sure your
shoulders stay relaxed and level with the ground [18, 19].
*During this phase, constantly remind yourself of the fundamental principles we
discussed previously: keep your head suspended (Hư lĩnh đỉnh kình), sink the chest and
raise the back (Hàm hung bạt bối), and let every movement originate from the waist [20-
22].*

**Phase 4: Cool-Down & Meditation (10 Minutes)**
* **Spinal Stretches & Swaying:** Perform gentle torso sways or forward folds to release
any accumulated tension in your lower back and hamstrings [23, 24]. * **Gathering the
Qi:** Stand with your feet together. Inhale as you sweep your arms up and around to
gather energy, then exhale as you guide your hands down the center of your body, settling
the energy back into your Dan Tian (about two inches below the navel) [25]. *
**Stillness:** Finish with a few minutes of seated or standing meditation to calm the
nervous system and absorb the benefits of the practice [8].
**Crucial Tips for Your Practice:**
* **Slow Down:** Rushing is a common beginner mistake that ruins alignment and
balance. Practice individual postures at 50–60% of your normal speed to build muscle
memory [12, 26]. * **Protect Your Knees:** Always make sure your knees point in the
same direction as your toes [14]. When bending, your knee should never extend past the
tip of your toes to avoid ligament strain [27]. * **Breathe Naturally:** Do not hold your
breath. Exhale when expanding or pushing out, and inhale when contracting or gathering
in [28, 29].
