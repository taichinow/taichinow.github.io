EN-101: The Complete Energetic Anatomy — From Wuji to Acupoint

1. Executive Summary & The Whole Map

Most qigong students hit the same wall: they memorise the names of hundreds of concepts — Qi, Yin–Yang, Five Phases, Three Treasures, Dantian, Meridian, Acupoint — but possess no map that connects them into a single system. The result is fragmented knowledge: one breathing session, one form, one acupressure point, each a separate shard, no shard fitting another. This article is that map.

The central thesis is simple: the energetic anatomy of Chinese medicine has a strict hierarchy. Every tier generates the next, and every tier carries its own clinical function. From abstract to concrete:

Wuji → Taiji → Yin–Yang → Five Phases → Bagua → Jing–Qi–Shen (Three Treasures) → Three Dantians → Eight Extraordinary Vessels → Twelve Primary Channels → Zang-Fu Organs → Sinew Channels / Luo Vessels → Acupoints → Chakras.

Why does this order matter? Because it dictates the order of practice. You cannot "open a point" before a channel exists to carry it; you cannot lead Qi through a channel before a Dantian field exists; you cannot gather Qi at the Dantian before distinguishing Qi from Shen. Students burn out because they work the tiers backwards. The hierarchy is the curriculum.

Clinical goal in one sentence: know which tier of the system a movement, a symptom, or a practice session lives on — so you can adjust at the correct tier instead of guessing.

2. The Metaphysical Ground: Wuji, Taiji, and the Birth of Duality

Before any meridian exists, the classics posit a primordial state: Wuji (無極) — "without ridgepole," undifferentiated, no opposition, no movement. In modern terms this is not a "void" but an undivided whole — the entire information of the system in a homogeneous state.

From Wuji, Taiji (太極) emerges as the ignition point of differentiation. The classics say "Taiji gives birth to the Two Modes" — the two modes being Yin (陰) and Yang (陽): two phases of one process, not two substances. Yin is the phase of contraction, gathering, cooling, stillness, inward direction; Yang is the phase of expansion, release, warming, movement, outward direction.

The misreading every student makes: Yin–Yang is not a thing cut into two halves, but two phases of one oscillation. An inhalation (Yang, intake) necessarily demands an exhalation (Yin, release). An opening movement (Yang) necessarily returns to closing (Yin). This is why the first exercise of every internal art is not a form but Wuji Standing (無極樁) — standing still in the pre-differentiated state, sensing the Yin–Yang rhythm of breath and weight before trying to control it.

3. The Five Phases: The Body's Generate–Control Algorithm

As Yin–Yang differentiates further, the classics describe the Five Phases (五行) — Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, Water. These are not "five material elements" in the Greek sense but five process phases, each describing a characteristic mode of energy-and-matter movement.

What makes the Five Phases an algorithm (and this is what Johnson emphasises) is its two relation cycles:

  • Sheng (相生) — generating cycle: Wood generates Fire, Fire generates Earth, Earth generates Metal, Metal generates Water, Water generates Wood. This is the nourishing cycle.
  • Ke (相剋) — controlling cycle: Wood controls Earth, Earth controls Water, Water controls Fire, Fire controls Metal, Metal controls Wood. This is the restraining cycle.

Because every Phase is bound to an Organ, a season, a direction, an emotion, a taste and a colour, the system becomes a diagnostic lookup table. Knowing the Liver (Wood) is excessive, the practitioner immediately knows it is over-controlling the Spleen (Earth) — and the digestive symptom is a consequence, not a cause. This is why, in traditional medicine, treating the stomach sometimes must begin with the liver.

4. The Three Treasures: Jing–Qi–Shen and Three Reserve Tiers

If the Five Phases describe the quality of energy, the Three Treasures (三寶) — Jing, Qi, Shen — describe its three levels of subtlety. This is the vertical axis of the entire energetic anatomy.

  • Jing (精) — essence; the inherited, stored material substrate. Jing sits at the lowest tier, bound to the kidneys, marrow, bone and reproductive capacity. It is the "raw fuel" — hard to replenish, easy to deplete, and it sets the baseline lifespan.
  • Qi (氣) — circulating energy; the refinement of Jing combined with breath and food. Qi is "fuel currently burning" — it circulates, regulates and protects.
  • Shen (神) — spirit; consciousness, awareness, clarity. Shen is "the bright flame" — it directs, orients and perceives.

These three tiers stand in an alchemical relation: Jing refines to Qi, Qi refines to Shen, Shen returns to emptiness. This is precisely the internal-alchemy (Neidan) sequence. Clinically it means you cannot force Shen to settle while Qi is chaotic, and you cannot calm Qi while Jing is exhausted. The therapeutic order runs bottom-up: nourish Jing (sleep, diet, moderation), regulate Qi (breath, movement), then settle Shen (meditation, stillness).

Johnson stresses that Yuan Qi (元氣) — the congenital Qi derived from parental Jing, which only depletes and never fully regenerates — is the reserve that all longevity practice aims to preserve, not refill. This is why the internal arts emphasise moderation over stimulation.

5. The Three Dantians: Three Energy Furnaces on the Body's Axis

Jing, Qi and Shen need dwelling places. Those places are the Three Dantians (三丹田) — three "elixir fields" stacked along the body's axis:

  • Lower Dantian (下丹田) — the lower abdomen, three finger-widths below the navel. The storehouse of Jing and Yuan Qi. The root of all movement and the body's centre of mass.
  • Middle Dantian (中丹田) — the mid-chest, at the Shanzhong point. The storehouse of post-heaven Qi, the furnace of breath and emotion. Also called "Sea of Qi."
  • Upper Dantian (上丹田) — from between the eyebrows (Yintang) to the crown (Baihui). The storehouse of Shen, where consciousness resides.

The three Dantians are not three points on the skin but three fields — volumetric regions with energy gradients. Cultivation means nourishing them in order: gather Qi at the Lower, lead it up to the Middle, raise Shen to the Upper. This is why all foundational practice starts at the belly — not because the belly is "sacred," but because it is the raw-fuel tier that must fill before the flame can rise.

6. The Eight Extraordinary Vessels: Eight Primary Currents

Before the Twelve Primary Channels (the conduits of the organs), the classics describe the Eight Extraordinary Vessels (奇經八脈) — eight "extraordinary" vessels running along the axis and outer circuit, belonging to no single organ. They are the foundation — generated first — and the Twelve Primary Channels overlay them.

The two most important are Ren Mai (任脈) and Du Mai (督脈):

  • Ren Mai — the front vessel (Yin), running from the perineum up to the lower lip, the standard of the "Sea of Yin." Linked to gestation, reproduction and blood.
  • Du Mai — the back vessel (Yang), running from the perineum up the spine to the crown and down to the upper lip, the standard of the "Sea of Yang." Linked to marrow, brain and spine.

The Lesser Heavenly Circuit (小周天) — the circulatory loop between Ren and Du — is the foundational internal-alchemy exercise: lead Qi up the Du Mai (back) and down the Ren Mai (front) into a closed loop. The other six vessels pair by the "open-close" (kai-he) principle, alternating in cycle.

7. The Twelve Primary Channels and Zang-Fu: The Distribution System

Over the Extraordinary Vessel foundation sits the main system: the Twelve Primary Channels (十二正經) — twelve primary conduits, six Yin and six Yang, each bound to a Yin Organ or Yang Organ. This is the distribution network: where Qi from the Dantian is divided out to the body's regions for nourishment and defence.

Structural rules:

  • Three Yang Arm channels (Large Intestine, Triple Burner, Small Intestine) run from hand to head.
  • Three Yang Leg channels (Stomach, Gallbladder, Bladder) run from head to foot.
  • Three Yin Leg channels (Spleen, Liver, Kidney) run from foot to chest.
  • Three Yin Arm channels (Lung, Pericardium, Heart) run from chest to hand.

This Yin–Yang handoff creates a closed circulation loop of Qi through the day. The clinically striking point is that each channel has a two-hour peak (its "hour of vigour"), when its flow is strongest — the basis of the "meridian clock" that modern medicine now recognises as bearing substantial similarities to circadian rhythm.

Yin Organs (store functions) and Yang Organs (transform functions) form matched Zang-Fu pairs (e.g. Heart–Small Intestine, Liver–Gallbladder, Spleen–Stomach). The pairing is not anatomical but a functional symbiosis — disease of the organ often manifests in its paired bowel, and vice versa.

8. Sinew Channels, Luo Vessels and Acupoints: Three Structural Tiers of a Channel

Meridians are not the thin lines drawn on a chart. In Johnson's account, each "channel" is really three structural tiers stacked together:

  • Sinew Channels (經筋) — the fascia tier: superficial planes and connective-tissue pathways beneath the skin. This is the tier that push-hands (tuishou) and body sensing interact with directly.
  • Luo Vessels (絡脈) — the transverse branches connecting one channel to another, forming a three-dimensional mesh. The Luo Vessels are where Qi "branches" and coordinates between organ systems.
  • Acupoints (穴道) — the nodal points of concentrated energy along the channel, where Qi gathers. Points are the control points of the whole system — external intervention can adjust the flow within.

These three tiers explain why so many methods act on the same channel: rubbing, pressing, stretching, rotating, breathing, intent-leading — each enters through a different tier. Beginners often try to "stimulate a point" while ignoring the Sinew Channels — yet in reality stretching fascia is the gentlest way to open a path for Qi without ever needing a needle.

The crucial practical point: an acupoint is not a point on the skin but a point in a chain of tissue pressure. When Qi moves correctly, the point becomes "full" and soft under the finger; when blocked, it is taut and tender. This is biological feedback, not belief.

9. Chakras and the Final Tier Integration

At the end of the hierarchy, Johnson cross-references the Indian system — the Chakras — seven centres along the spine. The essential point is that there is no need to homogenise the two systems: the Chakras and the Dantians/Acupoints are two ways of mapping the same vertical axis. The Lower Dantian corresponds to the lower chakras (Muladhara–Svadhisthana–Manipura); the Middle Dantian to Anahata–Vishuddha; the Upper Dantian to Ajna–Sahasrara. The correspondence is not exact — and the inexactness is itself data, revealing that the two systems were built for different ends: medicine (diagnosis and treatment) and spiritual cultivation (liberation).

This completes the hierarchical map: we have travelled from Wuji (most abstract) down to Acupoints and Chakras (most concrete, manipulable by hand). Every practice session sits somewhere on this axis. Counting breaths to settle intent → the Shen tier. Keeping the belly low during weight transfer → the Dantian/Qi tier. Stretching hip fascia → the Sinew Channel tier. Pressing Hegu for pain → the Acupoint tier. Knowing which tier you are on is 90% of practising correctly.
