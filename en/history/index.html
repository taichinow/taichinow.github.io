History of Tai Chi Chuan

From Zhang Sanfeng and Chen Village to the five modern schools

Legend and Recorded History

Zhang Sanfeng and the Snake vs. Crane (張三豐)

Legend holds that Zhang Sanfeng (張三豐) — a Daoist sage of the Song or Ming dynasty — founded Tai Chi Chuan after watching a fight between a snake and a crane. He saw that the snake's softness, agility and adaptability could defeat the crane's rigid strength. From this, the principle "yi rou ke gang" — soft overcomes hard — became the soul of the art.

Recorded Origins at Chen Village

Modern martial historians trace Tai Chi Chuan more concretely to Chenjiagou (陳家溝) in Wen County, Henan Province, in the 17th century. Chen Wangting (陳王廷, 1600–1680) — 9th generation of the Chen family — is recognized as the figure who synthesized inherited boxing with Qi cultivation, I Ching theory and Daoist philosophy into a coherent system. From the Chen school, the four other major schools later branched out.

Key Generations of the Chen Family

  • Chen Wangting (陳王廷) (9th gen., 1600–1680) — Founder; created seven form sets including the foundational La Jin, Lu Jin and silk-reeling patterns.
  • Chen Changxing (14th gen., ~1771–1853) — Taught Yang Luchan, who brought Tai Chi to Beijing.
  • Chen Fake (陳發科) (17th gen., 1887–1957) — Brought the art to Beijing and the wider world; attracted many non-family students.
  • Chen Xiaowang (陳小旺) (19th gen., b. 1945) — Global ambassador; taught in more than 30 countries.
  • Chen Xin (陳鑫) (16th gen., 1849–1929) — Author of Tai Chi Chuan Xiang Zhi Jie Mi Yi; the source of the famous line "Tai Chi Chuan is the method of silk-reeling."

The Five Major Schools

Today, Tai Chi Chuan has five major schools formally recognized by UNESCO as Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2020. Each has distinct characteristics but shares the same philosophical foundation:

  • Chen style (陳式) — The oldest school. Combines slow, soft movements with sudden bursts of power (fa jin). Spiral movements (silk reeling) and low stances are characteristic.
  • Yang style (楊式) — The most popular in the world, developed by Yang Luchan (1799–1872). Large, open movements at even pace; prioritizes health and meditation over combat.
  • Wu style (吳式) — Developed by Wu Quanxu. Small steps and refined movements; emphasizes balance and coordination.
  • Wu (Hao) style (武式) — The most compact school; focuses on internal principles, drawing power from the body's center.
  • Sun style (孫式) — Integrates Xingyiquan and Bagua Zhang; emphasizes flexible footwork and natural breathing rhythm.

Old Frame and New Frame

Within Chen style there are two canonical lineages:

  • Old Frame (Lao Jia · 老架) — Larger circles, pronounced silk reeling, lower stances. The form preserved from earlier generations.
  • New Frame (Xin Jia · 新架) — Smaller circles, subtler silk reeling, higher stances, more fa jin. Reorganized by Chen Fake in the early 20th century.

Tai Chi in the Modern World

From the parks of Beijing to medical centers in Boston, from hospitals in London to studios in Hanoi, hundreds of millions of people practice Tai Chi Chuan every day. The World Health Organization has included it among recommended physical activities for older adults. Thousands of scientific studies have confirmed its benefits for cardiovascular health, the nervous system, the bones and joints, and mental well-being.
