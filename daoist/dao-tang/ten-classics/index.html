The Ten Classics of the Daozang

An anthology of the Taoist Canon, with translations

Ten texts that carry the weight of the whole canon — philosophy, internal alchemy, liturgy and ethics — each shown in the original Chinese beside an English rendering.

How these ten were chosen

The Daozang holds roughly 1,476 distinct texts. Any selection of ten is an argument, not a fact, so the criteria are worth stating plainly. A text was included if it meets at least two of:

  • Canonical standing — it stands at the head of its section, or is the section's defining work.
  • Historical priority — it is the earliest surviving systematic statement of its kind.
  • Transmission — it shaped later practice across schools, not just within one lineage.
  • Relevance to internal arts — it is a source for the theory behind neigong and taijiquan.

The result deliberately spans the canon's three registers: philosophy (Daode Jing, Zhuangzi), internal alchemy (Cantong Qi, Wuzhen Pian, Huangting Jing, Yinfu Jing), liturgy and ethics (Duren Jing, Ganying Pian, Qingjing Jing), and compendium (Baopuzi).

A note on the translations. Every excerpt below is translated from the Chinese original printed immediately above it — never from another English version. The originals were taken from the Ming Zhengtong Daozang text as transcribed on Chinese Wikisource. These are working translations for practitioners and general readers, not a critical scholarly edition; where a term is contested I have kept the Chinese in parentheses rather than pretend to settle it.

The anthology

01 · 洞真部 本文類 · Dongzhen Section, Original Texts

元始無量度人上品妙經

The Wondrous Book of the Supreme Grade of the Primal Beginning's Salvation of Humanity

Known simply as the Duren Jing (度人經, "Scripture of Salvation"), this is the text the Ming editors placed at the very head of the Daozang. Its first juan is one of the ancient Lingbao scriptures of the Eastern Jin–Southern Dynasties; the remaining sixty juan are later accretions by Northern Song Shenxiao Daoists. It is chanted liturgically, and its eight-character motto — the Way of the Immortals prizes life — became a defining Daoist self-description against Buddhist renunciation of the body.

道言：昔於始青天中，碧落空歌，大浮黎土。受元始度人，無量上品，元始天尊，當說是經。

The Way speaks: In former times, in the Heaven of Primal Azure, in the Blue-Falling Void-Song, in the Great Fulai Realm, was received the Primal Beginning's Salvation of Humanity, Wondrous Book of the Supreme Grade — when the Celestial Worthy of Primal Beginning was about to expound this scripture.

仙道貴生，無量度人。

The Way of the Immortals prizes life, and saves humanity without measure.

Juan 1, 元始无量度人上品妙經 (scripture text)

02 · 太玄部 · Taixuan Section

道德經

The Classic of the Way and Its Virtue (Daode Jing)

Attributed to Laozi (6th–4th c. BCE), the eighty-one chapters that open the Taixuan section and, in practice, the whole canon. It is worth remembering that the Daode Jing is not merely "philosophy" sitting outside religion — it was canonised as scripture, and Tang emperors commissioned imperial commentaries on it. The two passages below supply the two poles of Daoist thought: the ineffability of the Way, and the doctrine that the Way models itself on nothing beyond its own spontaneity (ziran 自然).

道可道，非常道，名可名，非常名。

The Way that can be spoken is not the constant Way; the name that can be named is not the constant name.

人法地，地法天，天法道，道法自然。

Man follows Earth; Earth follows Heaven; Heaven follows the Way; the Way follows its own spontaneity.

Chapter 1 and Chapter 25 (Wang Bi recension)

03 · 太玄部 · Taixuan Section

南華真經

The True Classic of Southern Florescence (Zhuangzi)

The book of Zhuang Zhou (4th c. BCE). In 742 the Tang emperor Xuanzong conferred on Zhuangzi the title Nanhua Zhenren — "True Man of Southern Florescence" — which is why the canon lists it under that name rather than as 莊子. Of the thirty-three surviving chapters, the seven "Inner Chapters" are held to be Zhuangzi's own. For practitioners, the Inner Chapters are the deepest source on wu wei as a bodily skill rather than a doctrine: Cook Ding's knife, the swimmer in the waterfall, the cicada-catcher.

吾生也有涯，而知也无涯。以有涯隨无涯，殆已。

My life has a limit, but knowledge has none. To pursue the limitless with the limited — that is perilous indeed.

北冥有魚，其名爲鯤。

In the Northern Darkness there is a fish, and its name is Kun.

天地與我並生，而萬物與我爲一。

Heaven and Earth were born together with me, and the ten thousand things and I are one.

養生主 "Nourishing the Lord of Life"; 逍遙遊 "Free and Easy Wandering"; 齊物論 "On the Equality of Things"

04 · 洞真部 本文類 · Dongzhen Section, Original Texts

黃帝陰符經

The Hidden Talisman Classic of the Yellow Emperor (Yinfu Jing)

A short, dense text in three parts, of unknown authorship — variously dated to the Warring States, to the Northern Wei Daoist Kou Qianzhi, or to the Tang figure Li Quan. Its opening line is among the most quoted sentences in Chinese thought, and its doctrine of the "three thefts" (三盜) became fundamental to internal alchemy, where the mutual plundering of Heaven, Earth and human being describes the mechanism of cultivation itself.

觀天之道，執天之行，盡矣。

Observe the Way of Heaven; grasp the workings of Heaven — that is all.

天地，萬物之盜；萬物，人之盜；人，萬物之盜。三盜既宜，三才既安。

Heaven and Earth steal from the ten thousand things; the ten thousand things steal from man; man steals from the ten thousand things. When the three thefts are properly ordered, the Three Powers are at peace.

神仙抱一演道章上 (upper chapter); 富國安民演法章中 (middle chapter)

05 · 洞玄部 本文類 · Dongxuan Section, Original Texts

太上黃庭內景玉經

The Jade Classic of the Inner Landscape of the Yellow Court (Huangting Jing)

Transmitted, by tradition, through the female Daoist Wei Huacun (251–334). The Huangting Jing maps the body as a landscape inhabited by named deities, and its thirty-six chapters are the single most important scriptural source for the inner landscape chart (Neijing Tu) and for the whole "visualisation of the body" tradition that later internal alchemy inherited. The passage naming the spirits of the organs is the direct ancestor of much neigong instruction.

上清紫霞虛皇前，太上大道玉晨君，閑居蘂珠作七言，散化五形變萬神，是為《黃庭》作內篇。

Before the Void Sovereign of the purple cloud of Highest Clarity, the Lord of the Great Way, Jade Dawn, dwelling at leisure in the Jeweled Pearl, composed these seven-character lines; dispersing and transforming the five forms, he made the ten thousand spirits — this is the Inner Chapters of the Yellow Court.

心神丹元字守靈，肺神皓華字虛成，肝神龍煙字含明⋯六腑五臟神體精，皆在心內運天經，晝夜存之自長生。

The spirit of the heart is Cinnabar Origin, styled Guarding the Numinous; the spirit of the lungs is Bright Flower, styled Void Completion; the spirit of the liver is Dragon Mist, styled Embracing Brightness… The six bowels and five organs are the spirit-bodies' essence, all revolving within the heart along the celestial conduits; preserve them day and night, and you will of yourself attain long life.

上清章第一 (ch. 1) and 心神章第八 (ch. 8)

06 · 太清部 · Taiqing Section

周易參同契

The Seal of the Unity of the Three, According to the Book of Changes

By Wei Boyang (Eastern Han, 2nd c. CE), and honoured as the king of alchemical scriptures (萬古丹經王). Its innovation was to read the Yijing as an account of the alchemical process: trigrams become reagents, the lunar cycle becomes the firing schedule. Everything later written on neidan — Wuzhen Pian included — is in conversation with this book. The opening chapter sets out its operators: Qian and Kun as the frame, Kan and Li as the mechanism.

乾坤者，易之門戶，眾卦之父母。坎離匡廓，運轂正軸，牝牡四卦，以為橐籥。

Qian and Kun are the gate and door of the Changes, the father and mother of all the hexagrams. Kan and Li form the frame and enclosure, turning the hub and setting the axle true; these four hexagrams, female and male, serve as the bellows.

大易總敘章第一 (Chapter 1, "General Account of the Great Changes")

07 · 洞真部 · Dongzhen Section (Northern Song)

悟真篇

Chapters on Awakening to the Real (Wuzhen Pian)

By Zhang Boduan (983–1082), the founding text of the Southern School of internal alchemy. Zhang wrote eighty-one poems modelled on the Yijing's numbers, and his preface is a remarkable document of frustration: he complains that the classical sources speak endlessly of mercury and lead without ever saying what real lead and real mercury are. His famous line about planting a lotus in fire encodes the doctrine of "inverted" (顛倒) operation — seeking the yang within yin, the real within the phenomenal — which is the technical heart of neidan.

不識玄中顛倒顛，爭知火裏好栽蓮。

If you do not understand the inversion within the mystery, how will you know that a lotus may be planted in fire?

人身難得，光景易遷，罔測短脩，安逃業報。

A human body is hard to obtain, and time shifts easily; the length of one's span is unfathomable — how then to escape the recompense of one's deeds?

From the 絕句 (quatrains) and the author's preface (序)

08 · 太清部 · Taiqing Section

抱朴子內篇

The Master Who Embraces Simplicity — Inner Chapters (Baopuzi Neipian)

By Ge Hong (283–343), written in retirement on Mount Luofu. The twenty Inner Chapters are the earliest surviving systematic defence and description of the quest for immortality: its methods, its pharmacology, its arguments against sceptics. Ge Hong is the indispensable witness to what pre-Tang Daoist practice actually looked like before internal alchemy reorganised the field — and his first chapter, "Expansive Mystery" (暢玄), gives the metaphysical ground for the whole enterprise.

抱朴子曰：「玄者，自然之始祖，而萬殊之大宗也。」

The Master Who Embraces Simplicity said: "The Mysterious (玄) is the first ancestor of nature, and the great progenitor of the ten thousand differences."

夫金丹之為物，燒之愈久，變化愈妙。

As for the golden elixir: the longer it is fired, the more marvellous its transformations.

內篇卷一 暢玄 ("Expansive Mystery"); 內篇卷四 金丹 ("The Golden Elixir")

09 · 洞玄部 戒律類 · Dongxuan Section, Precepts

太上感應篇

The Most High's Treatise on Response and Retribution (Ganying Pian)

A Song-dynasty morality text of unknown authorship, and by a wide margin the most widely circulated Daoist scripture in Chinese history — printed, recited and distributed by the million. Its doctrine of ganying (感應, "stimulus and response") holds that Heaven operates not by arbitrary decree but by an automatic moral resonance: deeds summon their own recompense. It is the ethical backbone of popular Daoism, and the ancestor of the morality books (shanshu 善書) tradition.

太上曰：「禍福無門，惟人自召；善惡之報，如影隨形。」

The Most High says: "Calamity and blessing have no gate of their own — men call them forth themselves. The recompense of good and evil follows a man as a shadow follows its shape."

是道則進，非道則退。不履邪徑，不欺暗室；積德累功，慈心於物。

Advance along the Way; retreat from what is not the Way. Do not tread the crooked path; do not deceive in a dark room. Accumulate virtue and pile up merit; let your heart be compassionate toward all creatures.

Opening lines and the catalogue of virtues

10 · 洞神部 本文類 · Dongshen Section, Original Texts

太上老君說常清靜經

The Most High Lord Lao's Scripture of Constant Clarity and Stillness (Qingjing Jing)

A short scripture, probably of Tang date, that gives Daoist meditation its cleanest statement of method. Its sequence — desire stilled, heart settled, spirit clarified, then the systematic dissolution of subject and object in insight — is close enough to Buddhist śūnyatā analysis that the text has often been read as a Daoist answer to it. For practitioners it remains the most practical text in this list: the threefold contemplation (內觀/外觀/遠觀) is a complete instruction.

大道無形，生育天地；大道無情，運行日月；大道無名，長養萬物。吾不知其名，強名曰道。

The Great Way has no form, yet it gives birth to Heaven and Earth. The Great Way has no feeling, yet it moves the sun and moon. The Great Way has no name, yet it nourishes the ten thousand things. I do not know its name; forced to name it, I call it the Way.

人能常清靜，天地悉皆歸。

If a person can be constantly clear and still, Heaven and Earth will both return to him.

內觀於心，心無其心；外觀於形，形無其形；遠觀於物，物無其物。三者既悟，唯見於空。

Looking inward at the heart, the heart is no heart; looking outward at the form, the form is no form; looking afar at things, things are no things. When these three are understood, one sees only emptiness.

Opening; the doctrine of constant clarity; the threefold contemplation

Sources and method

The Chinese originals were transcribed from the Ming Zhengtong Daozang (正統道藏) as digitised on Chinese Wikisource, which records each text's canonical section. Section attributions above follow those colophons — for example the Yinfu Jing is there noted as coming from 洞真部 本文類 of the Zhengtong Daozang, and the Huangting Jing from 洞玄部 本文類.

Translations are literal in the first instance and literary only where literalness would mislead. Terms of art are given with their Chinese: 玄 as "the Mysterious", 橐籥 as "bellows", 顛倒 as "inversion", 三盜 as "the three thefts". Where a passage is disputed, that is noted rather than smoothed over.

On the limits of this page. These are selected excerpts, not complete translations. A full English rendering of all ten texts would run to several thousand pages; this anthology is designed to let a reader meet each text at its most characteristic moment, with the original present to check against.
