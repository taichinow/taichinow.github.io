Spine — On Axis, Never Straight

Region 2: the spine. Four curves, load-bearing function, and why flattening the back destroys balance.

Body MechanicsHead & NeckSpineThoraxShoulder GirdleElbow & WristWaist & PelvisKua & KneeFoot & AnkleThe Waist Program

Structure

The spine is the pillar of the trunk: 26 movable vertebrae, strong but flexible, sheathing the cord, joining head, thorax and pelvis, anchoring ribs and back muscles. Its primary function is bearing load; movement is secondary.

From the side there are four curves: cervical (7, forward), thoracic (12, backward), lumbar (5, forward), sacrococcygeal (backward). They add strength, keep balance in standing, absorb shock from the legs, and protect the spine from fracture.

So the spine should be upright but never straight. Any attempt to level the vertebrae or pull the spine into a line makes it rigid and badly compromises balance.

Principle

立身中正 — “stand centred and upright”; and 尾閭中正 — the tailbone held in centre.

“The head should be raised as if suspended from above by a thread. Thus the spine will be straight without being entirely straight, flexible without being stiff.” — Complete Tai-Chi

“Only with the central axis unbent can one support all eight directions.” — Fu Zhongwen

In the Chen line the waist and spine are called the first rulers: the waist is the axis of left-right turning, the spine the foundation of up-down bending.

Cues & Gate

Cue: “Long, not stiff.” When a student pulls the shoulders down too hard: “Spine long, shoulders drop.”

Region 2 gate

  • Three-point wall: occiput, thoracic back and sacrum all touch; the gap does not change on a deep inhale (if the chest leaves the wall, that is a region 3 fault);
  • Hinge and return: fold at the hips and stand up without adding a slump;
  • Turn: turn both ways with the tailbone still centred.

All three → region 3.
